# Replication Crisis

## Definition
The replication crisis concerns whether reported findings survive when their methods are re-run independently. In this vault the concept is now anchored on named replication projects rather than commentary: what matters is the study type, the sampled literature, the replication method, and the endpoint (significance, effect-size ratio, confidence-interval overlap), not the label "crisis."

## Anchor sources
- [[sources/osc-2015-estimating-reproducibility-psychological-science]] — the inspectable anchor. A multi-lab direct-replication project of 100 psychology studies (2008, three journals): replication effect sizes were half the originals, 36% of replications were statistically significant, and 97% of the originals had been. This is the vault's model of what a replication claim should look like.
- [[sources/begley-ellis-2012-raise-standards-preclinical-cancer]] — the cancer-biology lead. A Nature commentary reporting that Amgen confirmed 6 of 53 landmark preclinical cancer studies in internal replications (about 11%). The underlying per-study data were not published, so the figure is a reported experience, not an inspectable replication study. Treat as unresolved evidence.
- [[sources/baker-2016-nature-reproducibility-survey]] — the attitude survey. 1,576 researchers: most reported failed replication attempts (of others' and their own work), most still trusted the literature, and the social machinery for surfacing failures was weak. Surveys cannot establish replication rates.
- [[sources/scientism-conversation-may-1-2026]] — the seed source that motivated the vault's interest; Tier 3, generates questions only.

## What the anchors show, and what they do not
- There is direct, published evidence that a defined sample of psychology findings mostly did not replicate under independent re-runs. That claim is field-specific and date-specific.
- There is reported but not inspectable evidence that landmark preclinical cancer findings rarely survived industrial validation.
- There is survey evidence that researchers widely experience replication failure and rarely publish or discuss it.
- None of these sources measures "science as a whole." Anyone extending them to a field-wide or all-science quantified claim is making an inferential leap ([[concepts/inferential-leap]]).

## What this concept includes
- failed direct replications with named protocols ([[sources/osc-2015-estimating-reproducibility-psychological-science]])
- fragile results that depend on narrow analytic choices and selective reporting (the failure modes the Amgen report attributes its failures to)
- methods that are under-described or hard to reconstruct
- weak social machinery for surfacing failures ([[sources/baker-2016-nature-reproducibility-survey]])

## What this concept does not settle
- Replication alone does not resolve whether a broader interpretation is justified — a result can replicate and still be loaded with an interpretive overreach. That is [[concepts/inferential-leap]], kept separate.
- A failed replication is not proof the original claim is false; the OSC project's own structure (confidence-interval overlap, pooled analysis) exists because significance at p < 0.05 is a blunt endpoint.
- These anchors say nothing directly about biomedical claims audited elsewhere in this vault; for those, see [[synthesis/viral-disease-causation-evidence-ladder]], where replication is treated as the strongest rung of causal evidence.

## Main limitations
The anchors share a common boundary: none measures all of science. The OSC project is one field, one year, three journals; its failed replications are not falsifications. The Amgen figure is a commentary-level report whose underlying data were not released. The Nature survey is self-reported attitude data. Any use of this page must carry those limits with it.

## What this wiki currently concludes
- Direct replication projects in psychology produced a large measured replication shortfall: this is supported by the named project.
- The widely quoted "about 10% of cancer biology replicates" figure rests on a commentary whose data were not released: mark it unresolved whenever used.
- Attitude surveys document experience and practice patterns, not replication rates.
- Replication remains this vault's preferred marker of reliable experimental findings — which is exactly why unreplicated or uninspectable replication reports should not be inflated into stronger claims than they can carry.
