# Association of Virus with Cases of Rubella Studied in Toronto

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary rubella isolation, interference, serology, and monkey-inoculation paper
- Journal/year: *Canadian Medical Association Journal*, 1963
- Authors: Bergthora Sigurdardottir, Kathleen F. Givan, K. R. Rozee, and A. J. Rhodes
- Source basis in hand: full-text PDF rendered through Europe PMC/PMC, checked against PubMed/PMC metadata

## Vault extraction
| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Study type | Typical-rubella specimen culture, interference assay using ECHO-11 and other challenge viruses, paired-serum neutralization, physical-characterization tests, and grivet-monkey inoculation |
| Subject / sample | Throat washings and paired sera from cases of clinically typical rubella; two healthy African green monkeys for inoculation with isolate R23 |
| Exposure or intervention | Patient specimens were inoculated into African green monkey kidney cultures and tested for interference with challenge viruses; two grivet monkeys received seventh-passage R23 virus pool intraperitoneally and intranasally |
| Comparator / control | Uninoculated culture controls, challenge-virus controls, cultures inoculated with rubella specimen but not challenge virus, acute versus convalescent serum comparison, and monkey clinical observation before/after inoculation |
| Endpoint | Interference with cytopathology caused by ECHO-11 and other challenge viruses, neutralization by convalescent but not acute sera, heat and ether sensitivity, absence of visible cytopathology in unstained or stained cultures, monkey rash, lymphadenopathy, and white-blood-cell change |
| Result | Virus was isolated from 9 of 18 specimens from typical rubella cases. In 4 of 5 paired sera, convalescent but not acute serum neutralized the R23 isolate by interference inhibition. Two grivet monkeys inoculated with seventh-passage R23 material developed a mild illness characterized by macular rash and lymphadenopathy, without obvious fever or respiratory/enteric disturbance. |
| Main limitation | The monkey exposure combined intraperitoneal and intranasal inoculation in only two animals, so it is not ordinary human transmission. The isolation endpoint depended on interference assays rather than visible primary cytopathology. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that an interference-inducing agent was recovered from some typical rubella throat-washing specimens in the Toronto series.
- It can answer that convalescent sera from several cases neutralized the R23 isolate when acute sera did not under the reported assay.
- It can answer that a passaged isolate produced mild rash and lymphadenopathy in two grivet monkeys under combined intraperitoneal/intranasal inoculation.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot prove ordinary human-to-human transmission.
- It cannot show that grivet-monkey illness is identical to human rubella.
- It cannot prove congenital rubella syndrome.
- It cannot quantify infection risk or clinical disease risk in naturally exposed humans.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The paper is vulnerable to animal-model compression. A mild grivet-monkey response after combined artificial inoculation is relevant challenge evidence, but it should not be retold as natural human transmission or fetal disease proof.

## What this wiki currently concludes
This source supports a narrow claim: in the 1963 Toronto study, a rubella-associated interference agent was recovered from half of the typical-rubella specimens tested, was neutralized by convalescent sera in most paired tests, and produced mild clinical signs in two inoculated grivet monkeys.
