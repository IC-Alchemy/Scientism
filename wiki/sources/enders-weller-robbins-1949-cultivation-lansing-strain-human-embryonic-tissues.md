# Cultivation of the Lansing Strain of Poliomyelitis Virus in Cultures of Various Human Embryonic Tissues

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary experimental tissue-culture cultivation paper
- Journal/year: *Science*, 1949
- Authors: John F. Enders, Thomas H. Weller, Frederick C. Robbins

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that the Lansing strain retained or increased infectious activity in the reported human embryonic tissue-culture systems under the tested conditions.
- It can answer that non-neural embryonic arm-leg tissue cultures could support an agent that remained Lansing-like by mouse disease, specific antiserum neutralization, and selected rhesus-monkey challenge endpoints.
- It can answer that intestinal tissue culture produced mouse-infective material, while the paper itself kept that finding less complete because neutralization and monkey confirmation were not yet done for that tissue category.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot establish ordinary poliomyelitis causation in humans by natural exposure.
- It cannot show host-to-host transmission.
- It cannot show direct isolation from patients; the experiment used the Lansing strain rather than fresh patient specimens.
- It cannot by itself collapse tissue-culture growth, animal paralysis, and human epidemic causation into one endpoint.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The main risk is treating tissue-culture propagation as if it settled the full disease-causation question. This paper strengthens the laboratory cultivation rung of the polio evidence ladder, but it does not answer exposure route, natural host-to-host transmission, population susceptibility, or clinical-case attribution.

## What this wiki currently concludes
This source supports a narrow cultivation claim: in the reported 1949 experiments, a Lansing poliomyelitis-virus strain could be propagated in human embryonic tissue cultures, including non-neural arm-leg tissue cultures, and retained infectivity/identity by the endpoints tested. It should not be used as standalone proof of ordinary natural-route poliomyelitis causation.
