# Is Complete Purification/Isolation of a "Virus" Even Possible? - ViroLIEgy

> [!warning] Warning
> This source is advocacy commentary from ViroLIEgy. Its long quotations from a methods review are part of the source's claims and have not been verified against the original paper in this vault. The essay's framing and conclusion are the author's argument, not vault findings.

## Source type
Commentary essay by Mike Stone on ViroLIEgy, published 2021-09-20. The argument is carried mostly by block quotations from a peer-reviewed review of exosome recovery and purification methods. Tier 3.

## What the source claims
- The essay argues that completely purifying and isolating a suspected viral particle from an unaltered patient sample is practically impossible at nanoparticle scale, because particles that overlap in size, density, and appearance cannot be fully separated.
- It argues that "viruses" and exosomes are near-identical in size, shape, and appearance, noting exosomes have been called "non-infectious viruses," so the documented limits of exosome isolation apply to viral purification.
- Drawing on the quoted review, the essay reports that:
  - no exosome-isolation methodology provides sufficient robustness in purification yield, selectivity, and reproducibility
  - ultracentrifugation is the field's standard method, yet contamination from other vesicles, molecules, or particles that overlap in density and size is expected, and g-force and rotor choices materially affect purity and yield
  - ultrafiltration cannot isolate only exosomes, since microvesicles, apoptotic bodies, and abundant proteins of similar size or molecular weight co-isolate
  - precipitation methods yield high particle counts at low purity, with unavoidable co-isolation of lipoproteins, ribonucleoprotein complexes, albumin, immunoglobulins, and other vesicles, plus post-precipitation aggregation
- It concludes that virology has failed to demonstrate complete purification or isolation of any particle claimed to be a virus, and that no conclusion about any particle as a "virus" is justified until that step occurs.

## What questions it opens
- What would count as adequate isolation or purification of a virus, and is "complete" purity a coherent operational demand at nanoparticle scale? This methodological question is unresolved in this vault.
- Does the exosome and extracellular-vesicle overlap genuinely constrain the causation and genome-attribution claims made from purified-but-contaminated viral preparations?
- Lead to verify: the underlying methods review the essay quotes (identified in the raw file as PMC6972601, on recovery and purification of cellular exosomes). Ingest it directly and check whether the quoted passages are accurate and in context.
- Lead to verify: which specific virology papers claim purification, and by what operational endpoint (gradient fraction, particle count, sequencing depth), so the dispute can be judged rung by rung rather than all-or-nothing.

## What this wiki currently concludes
- This is Tier 3 material. It generates research leads and no factual conclusions.
- The essay's argument is recorded as its author's position. Whether complete purification is possible, impossible, or an ill-posed standard is unresolved in this vault; no named experiment in hand answers it. We do not know.
- The essay belongs to the same dispute structure as [[sources/has-the-virus-been-isolated-no-viroliegy]] and [[sources/the-virus-cult-a-religion-built-on-fear-not-science]]: a strict isolation standard is demanded, and current virological practice is argued not to meet it.
- If the quoted passages verify against the original review, they describe real separation limits for exosome-scale particles; that alone would still not settle what isolation standard virology requires for existence or causation claims.

## Methodology and evidence notes
- The quotations come from a review of exosome methods, not from experiments on viral purification. The transfer from exosomes to viruses is the author's inference; the claim that they are "identical in every way" is asserted in the essay, not experimentally demonstrated within it.
- No experiment, sample, endpoint, control, or result for a viral purification attempt is named in the essay.
- The standard demanded, complete separation from all other particles, is a definitional choice. The essay argues from logic ("logic does not deal with fairness") rather than from an experimental result, which is itself a candidate [[concepts/inferential-leap]] from methodological limits to a global negative conclusion.
