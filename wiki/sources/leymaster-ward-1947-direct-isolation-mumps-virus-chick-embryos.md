# Direct Isolation of Mumps Virus in Chick Embryos

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary direct-isolation and identification-method paper for mumps virus from patient saliva
- Journal/year: *Proceedings of the Society for Experimental Biology and Medicine*, 1947
- Authors: Glenn R. Leymaster and Thomas G. Ward
- Source basis in hand: full-text official journal PDF from *Experimental Biology and Medicine*, checked against DOI and PubMed metadata

## Vault extraction
| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Study type | Direct inoculation of acute mumps saliva into chick-embryo amniotic sacs, with hemagglutination, serial embryo passage, complement fixation, and confirmatory monkey inoculation |
| Subject / sample | Saliva from nine patients clinically diagnosed with mumps during the acute inflammatory stage |
| Exposure or intervention | Saliva was mixed with broth, centrifuged, treated with penicillin and streptomycin, and inoculated in 0.1 ml amounts into the amniotic sac of 7- or 8-day-old chick embryos |
| Comparator / control | Specimens were considered negative only after three embryo passages without hemagglutination; one clinically diagnosed patient with prior mumps had negative isolation and no convalescent complement-fixation rise, making the clinical diagnosis unconfirmed by the paper's laboratory endpoints |
| Endpoint | Hemagglutination in amniotic fluid, successful embryo passage, complement-fixation identification using human acute/convalescent mumps sera, monkey parotid-duct disease after inoculation, re-isolation from monkey parotid tissue, and monkey clinical/serologic response |
| Result | Mumps virus was isolated from 8 of 9 clinically diagnosed patients. The one negative case also lacked a convalescent complement-fixation rise and was treated as laboratory-unconfirmed despite clinical diagnosis. Two specimens were additionally tested in rhesus monkeys, where the authors reported parotid swelling, re-isolation from parotid tissue, and clinical/serologic evidence in surviving monkeys. |
| Main limitation | This is a laboratory-isolation and identification method, not a natural-transmission experiment. It used a small clinical sample, no healthy-saliva control series is reported in the extracted paper, and confirmatory monkey testing still used parotid-duct inoculation. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that saliva from most clinically diagnosed acute mumps patients in the tested set yielded an agent in chick-embryo amniotic-sac culture that met the paper's hemagglutination and complement-fixation identification endpoints.
- It can answer that the authors connected selected chick-embryo isolates back to the rhesus-monkey parotid endpoint and re-isolation workflow.
- It can answer that one clinical mumps diagnosis in the series was not confirmed by isolation or paired complement-fixation rise.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot prove ordinary human-to-human mumps transmission.
- It cannot prove that every parotitis case is caused by mumps virus.
- It cannot establish a natural route, dose, or timing of infection.
- It cannot by itself prove clinical causation; it primarily strengthens the isolation/identification rung.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The main risk is treating chick-embryo isolation from acute saliva as equivalent to full human causation. The paper is stronger than a mere clinical association because it includes propagation and identification endpoints, but the causal question still needs route, exposure, clinical outcome, serology, and transmission context.

## What this wiki currently concludes
This source supports a narrow claim: in Leymaster and Ward's 1947 experiment, acute mumps saliva from 8 of 9 clinically diagnosed patients yielded mumps-virus isolates by amniotic-sac chick-embryo inoculation under the authors' hemagglutination, complement-fixation, and selected monkey-confirmation criteria.
