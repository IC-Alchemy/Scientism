# A Virus Obtained from Influenza Patients

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary influenza-virus animal-identification paper
- Journal/year: *The Lancet*, 1933
- Authors: Wilson Smith, C. H. Andrewes, P. P. Laidlaw
- Source basis in hand: full-text PDF facsimile obtained from an online mirror, checked against DOI and ScienceDirect metadata

> Warning: The PDF is a facsimile of the 1933 Lancet pages and includes neighboring article text on the first page. The extraction below uses only the influenza article text.

## Vault extraction
| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Study type | Human-case specimen filtration, animal inoculation, serial passage, contact exposure, tissue recovery, and neutralization experiment |
| Subject / sample | Throat washings from eight human cases diagnosed as influenza; additional washings from four non-influenza or convalescent human subjects; ferrets as the main experimental host |
| Exposure or intervention | Bacteriologically sterile filtered throat washings or ferret nasal-turbinate material instilled intranasally into ferrets; initial successful experiment also included subcutaneous exposure |
| Comparator / control | Non-influenza/convalescent human washings and common-cold nasal secretion; neutralization controls using virus alone or virus mixed with normal serum; recovered-ferret serum and human convalescent serum tested for neutralizing activity |
| Endpoint | Ferret illness with temperature response and nasal catarrh; serial passage by intranasal transfer; contact transmission between ferrets; recovery of infective material from nasal passages; neutralization by immune serum |
| Result | Five of eight early influenza-case washings produced the described ferret disease. Twenty-six serial passages of one strain were reported with typical temperature response and symptoms. Contact transmission between ferrets was reported. Washings from healthy subjects, convalescents, and a common-cold subject were non-infective in the reported tests. Convalescent human sera and recovered-ferret sera neutralized the virus in some tests, while normal ferret serum did not. |
| Main limitation | Preliminary paper; no controlled human challenge; no direct proof that the same agent was sufficient for ordinary human influenza; route was primarily intranasal animal inoculation, and no route other than nasal inoculation was reported successful. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that some early human influenza-case throat washings contained a filterable, bacteriologically sterile agent capable of producing a reproducible ferret illness under the tested route.
- It can answer that the ferret agent could be passed serially through nasal-turbinate material and could spread by co-housing in ferrets.
- It can answer that the investigators recovered infective material from sick ferret nasal passages, but not from the other tissues they tested.
- It can answer that convalescent or immune sera could neutralize the ferret disease endpoint in the reported assay.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot establish ordinary human-to-human influenza transmission.
- It cannot prove that the ferret disease is identical to the whole clinical syndrome of human influenza.
- It cannot show that influenza virus alone is sufficient for every human influenza case or epidemic pattern.
- It cannot resolve Rosenau-style negative human transmission experiments, because it tested a later animal model and different specimen handling rather than repeating controlled human exposure.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The main risk is treating "a virus obtained from influenza patients" as if it by itself proved the full ordinary-causation ladder in humans. The paper supports the human-case filtrate -> ferret disease -> ferret passage rung. It does not collapse animal challenge, serology, human transmission, and population causation into a single endpoint.

## What this wiki currently concludes
This source supports a narrow claim: in the reported 1933 experiments, filtered material from some early human influenza cases produced a transmissible, serum-neutralizable ferret disease under intranasal experimental conditions. It should be read as a major influenza-virus identification rung, not as standalone proof of ordinary human influenza causation.
