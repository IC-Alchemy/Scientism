# Insusceptibility of Man to Inoculation with Blood from Measles Patients

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary human-volunteer negative-result paper for measles blood-inoculation claims
- Journal/year: *Bulletin of the Johns Hopkins Hospital*, 1919
- Author: Andrew Watson Sellards
- Source basis in hand: full text from Internet Archive volume OCR and PDF

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Human volunteer inoculation study |
| Subject / sample | Adult military volunteers screened for health and absence of recognized prior measles; blood from measles patients at various disease stages |
| Exposure or intervention | Injections of blood from measles patients; two blood-inoculated subjects were later inoculated on upper-respiratory mucosa with nasal secretions from an early measles case |
| Comparator / control | No randomized sham control; susceptibility was inferred from history and later exposure/inoculation checks, not from a guaranteed controlled challenge system |
| Endpoint | Clinical measles symptoms after inoculation, especially rash and systemic illness |
| Result | Sellards reported that blood injections into supposedly susceptible individuals produced no measles symptoms. He also reported that two blood-inoculated individuals later exposed on upper-respiratory mucosa to nasal secretions from an early case did not develop symptoms, which he treated cautiously as possible blood-induced protection rather than proof. |
| Main limitation | Volunteer susceptibility could not be guaranteed, natural exposure was not reconstructed, and the study tested blood inoculation more directly than respiratory spread. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that this human-volunteer series failed to produce clinical measles by inoculating blood from measles patients under the reported conditions.
- It can answer that the author considered prior measles history and respiratory-tract health important sources of uncertainty.
- It can answer that negative blood inoculation undermined simple retellings that tiny amounts of measles blood reliably produced measles in humans.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot prove that measles is not transmissible by respiratory secretions.
- It cannot identify or exclude a tissue-culture virus.
- It cannot separate true immunity, occult prior infection, and failed exposure in every volunteer.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The main risk is treating failure of blood inoculation as failure of the measles-virus hypothesis as a whole. The proper endpoint is narrower: blood did not produce clinical measles in the tested volunteer series.

## Vault conclusion
Sellards 1919 supports a human blood-inoculation negative result. It belongs beside, not instead of, respiratory-secretion, monkey, serology, and tissue-culture evidence.
