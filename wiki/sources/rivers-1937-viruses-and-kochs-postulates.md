# Viruses and Koch's Postulates

## TL;DR
Rivers' 1937 paper is a primary methodological source for viral disease causation. It argues that Koch's original bacteriology rules cannot be copied literally into virology, especially where viruses cannot be grown on lifeless media, but it does not replace causal proof with authority or loose association.

## Source type and scope
- Presidential address and journal article in *Journal of Bacteriology*.
- Historical-methodological source, not a disease-specific trial.
- Lens lookup: Lens `001-842-966-232-780`; DOI `10.1128/jb.33.1.1-12.1937`; PMID `16559982`; PMCID `PMC545348`.

## What the source claims
- Koch's postulates were important for bacteriology but can obstruct viral disease research if applied mechanically.
- Viral causation still requires showing that the virus is regularly associated with the disease and is not merely incidental, accidental, or a coexisting infection.
- For viruses, Rivers emphasizes lesion timing, susceptible hosts, infectious material free from ordinary microbes, controls, immunological studies, and avoidance of fortuitous association.
- Rivers explicitly warns that antibodies or virus recovery alone can mislead if the virus is merely carried alongside another disease process.

## What experiment or observation is involved
- This paper is not a single experiment.
- It is a historical methodological document that uses examples from viral research to propose how etiological reasoning should work when pure culture on lifeless media is unavailable.

## What question the paper is capable of answering
`What methodological standard did Rivers propose for judging viral disease causation when literal Koch postulates could not be satisfied?`

It is capable of clarifying the proof standard Rivers thought virology needed. It is not capable of proving any specific virus-disease pair by itself.

## What this wiki currently concludes
- This source is important because it shows that the viral-causation debate is not simply "Koch's postulates fulfilled or not fulfilled."
- Rivers preserves a stricter boundary than many casual summaries: a virus must be shown to be causally relevant, not merely present.
- The paper supports a cross-cutting audit rule for this vault: detection, isolation, antibody response, animal inoculation, and clinical disease should be recorded as distinct endpoints rather than collapsed into one word, "cause."
