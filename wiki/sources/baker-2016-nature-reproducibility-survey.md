# 1,500 scientists lift the lid on reproducibility

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary record of the largest researcher-attitude survey on reproducibility; useful for practice patterns, not for estimating what fraction of results are true
- Journal/year: *Nature* survey feature (Monya Baker), 2016; raw copy held in `raw/reprocrisis/`
- Source basis in hand: full extracted text in the raw vault, including the survey figures

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Online survey of researchers (attitudes and self-reported experiences), published with descriptive statistics |
| Subject / sample | 1,576 researchers who completed the survey across fields and career stages |
| Intervention or exposure | None — observational survey of self-reported experience |
| Comparator / control | Cross-tabulations by field and by agreement items; no counterfactual |
| Endpoint | Self-reported replication attempts and outcomes; agreement with crisis statements; trust in published literature; publishing and contact behavior around failed replications |
| Result | 52% agreed there is a significant crisis and 38% a slight crisis. More than 70% reported failing to reproduce another scientist's experiments; more than 50% failed to reproduce their own. Fewer than 31% thought a failure to reproduce a published result means it is probably wrong; 73% trusted at least half of the papers in their field. Fewer than 20% had ever been contacted by someone unable to reproduce their work, and a minority had tried to publish replication attempts. The article characterizes the best-known quantitative analyses as roughly 40% replication in psychology and about 10% in cancer biology, citing the OSC project and the Amgen experience. |
| Main limitation | Self-selected respondents; self-reported experiences; no definition of "reproducibility" was imposed, which the article itself flags ("no consensus on what reproducibility is"). Attitude data cannot establish what fraction of published results actually replicate. |

## What the survey can answer
- What researchers say about their own replication attempts and their trust in the literature at one point in time (2016).
- That failed replications are widely experienced but rarely surfaced — few researchers report being contacted about them or publishing them.

## What it cannot answer
- The true reproducibility rate of any literature. Only direct replication projects with published data — such as [[sources/osc-2015-estimating-reproducibility-psychological-science]] — can address that, field by field.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The leap is quoting "70% of researchers have failed to reproduce experiments" as if it were a measured replication rate of the literature. It is a self-reported experience survey. The two best-known replication-rate figures it relays differ in inspectability: the psychology project is a published data paper; the cancer-biology figure traces to a commentary ([[sources/begley-ellis-2012-raise-standards-preclinical-cancer]]) whose underlying data were not released.

## Vault conclusion
This source supports narrow attitudinal and practice-pattern claims: a majority of surveyed researchers reported failed replication attempts and a majority still trusted the literature, while the social machinery for surfacing failures (contact, publication) was weak. It cannot anchor any quantitative claim about how much of any literature replicates.
