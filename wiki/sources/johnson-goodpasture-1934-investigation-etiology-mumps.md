# An Investigation of the Etiology of Mumps

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary mumps-virus animal-challenge and route-boundary paper
- Journal/year: *Journal of Experimental Medicine*, 1934
- Authors: Claud D. Johnson and Ernest W. Goodpasture
- Source basis in hand: full-text PDF rendered through Europe PMC/PMC, checked against DOI and PubMed/PMC metadata

## Vault extraction
| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Study type | Human-case specimen inoculation into rhesus-monkey parotid glands, serial monkey passage, filterability tests, normal-saliva controls, reinoculation, and serum-inhibition experiments |
| Subject / sample | Fresh saliva from six human mumps cases in early disease; two normal human saliva specimens; *Macacus rhesus* monkeys |
| Exposure or intervention | Whole saliva or monkey parotid-gland emulsions inoculated directly into the parotid glands through Stensen's duct; filtered gland emulsions also tested |
| Comparator / control | Two monkeys received normal human saliva by the same parotid-duct method; previously infected monkeys were reinoculated while a normal monkey received the same gland emulsion; normal-serum mixtures were compared with convalescent mumps-serum mixtures |
| Endpoint | Monkey parotid swelling/tenderness, fever, leukopenia, facial edema, parotid weight and histopathology, bacterial sterility, filterability, serial passage, reinoculation resistance, and serum-inhibition signal |
| Result | Four of six early-case saliva specimens produced the experimental monkey parotitis; the two normal-saliva inoculated monkeys had only transient injection-related swelling and no later parotitis, fever, or leukopenia. One strain was passed through seven monkey generations. Bacterially sterile filtered gland emulsions remained virulent in five trials. Two recovered monkeys resisted reinoculation while a normal control monkey developed characteristic parotitis. The serum-inhibition tests were described by the authors as inconclusive but suggestive. |
| Main limitation | The central disease endpoint was produced by direct parotid-duct inoculation in monkeys, not by ordinary human exposure. Sample and control numbers were small, neutralization was not cleanly resolved, and the paper does not reconstruct natural human-to-human transmission. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that some saliva specimens from early human mumps cases contained an agent that produced acute non-suppurative parotitis in rhesus monkeys when placed directly into the parotid duct.
- It can answer that this experimental monkey lesion was passaged through monkey parotid tissue and that filtered gland emulsions retained activity under the reported conditions.
- It can answer that the same parotid-duct procedure using two normal human saliva specimens did not produce the delayed experimental parotitis endpoint.
- It can answer that recovered monkeys resisted reinoculation in the limited test, while a normal monkey control developed the experimental disease.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot prove ordinary human mumps transmission by normal social exposure.
- It cannot prove that direct parotid-duct inoculation is a natural route.
- It cannot show that mumps virus alone explains every clinical parotitis case.
- It cannot replace later direct isolation, serology, or controlled human exposure evidence.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The paper is vulnerable to artificial-route compression. A strong result by direct placement of case saliva or gland material into a rhesus-monkey parotid duct can be retold as if it had already settled ordinary human natural-route causation. Under this vault's standard, it supports the animal parotid-inoculation rung and a normal-saliva control boundary, not the entire mumps causation ladder.

## What this wiki currently concludes
This source supports a narrow claim: in Johnson and Goodpasture's 1934 experiments, early-case mumps saliva and passaged monkey parotid material produced a reproducible rhesus-monkey parotitis endpoint when inoculated through Stensen's duct. It should be used as a primary animal-model and route-boundary source, not as standalone proof of ordinary human mumps causation.
