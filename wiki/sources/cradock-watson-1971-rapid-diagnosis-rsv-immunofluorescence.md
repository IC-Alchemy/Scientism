# Rapid Diagnosis of Respiratory Syncytial Virus Infection in Children by the Immunofluorescent Technique

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary diagnostic antigen-detection and culture-comparison paper for RSV in hospitalized children
- Journal/year: *Journal of Clinical Pathology*, 1971
- Authors: J. E. Cradock-Watson, J. McQuillin, and P. S. Gardner
- Source basis in hand: full-text PMC article

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Diagnostic detection and culture-comparison study |
| Subject / sample | Children under 2 hospitalized with acute respiratory infection, predominantly lower respiratory tract disease |
| Exposure or intervention | Natural illness; nasopharyngeal secretions tested by immunofluorescence and culture |
| Comparator / control | Conventional cell culture from the same cases |
| Endpoint | RSV antigen in nasopharyngeal secretions by immunofluorescence and culture confirmation |
| Result | Among 238 infants with bronchiolitis, fluorescent diagnosis was made in 185, with 180 confirmed by culture. |
| Main limitation | Diagnostic association only; upper-airway detection/culture does not by itself prove lower-airway disease causation or sufficiency. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that RSV antigen detection in nasopharyngeal secretions aligned closely with culture confirmation in many hospitalized bronchiolitis cases.
- It can answer diagnostic performance in the sampled clinical setting.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot prove lower-airway pathogenesis or causal sufficiency by itself.
- It cannot separate asymptomatic carriage, timing, or all bronchiolitis mechanisms.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The risk is treating a strong diagnostic association as a complete bronchiolitis causation experiment. It is a detection/culture rung.

## Vault conclusion
Cradock-Watson et al. 1971 supports a strong RSV diagnostic association rung in hospitalized infants with bronchiolitis, while leaving causation and pathogenesis separate.
