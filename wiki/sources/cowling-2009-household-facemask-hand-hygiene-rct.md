# Facemasks and Hand Hygiene to Prevent Influenza Transmission in Households

## Source type
- Role in this vault: prospective cluster-randomized controlled trial of natural household influenza transmission with a laboratory-confirmed infection endpoint; fills GAP 2 (controlled natural-route transmission) in [[synthesis/source-verification-queue]] for influenza, the exposure route the human-challenge rung does not touch.
- Journal/year: *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2009;151(7):437-446 (published online 6 October 2009)
- Authors: Benjamin J. Cowling, Kwok-Hung Chan, Vicky J. Fang, Calvin K. Y. Cheng, Dennis K. M. Fung, Wai Wai, Eric Lau, Thomas So, Wayne Sin, Susan Seto, Honglin Yung, Wilson W. S. Chu, Brian C. W. Chiu, Kwok Keung Lee, Peter Chiu, Dennis K. Lee, Timothy M. Uyeki, Paul M. Houck, J. S. Malik Peiris, Gabriel M. Leung
- Trial registration: NCT00425893; primary funding source: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention
- Source basis in hand: full-text archived publisher HTML of the article page (annals.org HighWire full text captured 2009-10-18 and 2012-03-18 via the Internet Archive), including Methods, Results, and Discussion; the numbered data tables (Table 3, 4, 5) are linked objects and were not archived, so per-arm point estimates below are taken from the article narrative text.

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Prospective cluster-randomized controlled trial (household = cluster), 1:1:1 allocation with block randomization (block sizes 18/24/30), computer-generated allocation concealed from recruiting physicians and clinics; participants and intervention administrators unblinded; conducted in 45 Hong Kong outpatient clinics, 2 January - 30 September 2008 (interpandemic season) |
| Subject / sample | 2750 potential index patients screened from outpatient clinics; 407 index patients with influenza-like illness (>=2 symptoms, onset <48 h) and positive QuickVue Influenza A+B rapid test, plus 794 household contacts, randomized in 407 households; after protocol exclusions (16 households with unconfirmed index infection, 47 with contact PCR-positivity at baseline, dropouts), the primary analysis set was 259 households / 794 household contacts; index patients: 160 (62%) influenza A, 99 (38%) influenza B; about two-thirds of index patients were children |
| Intervention or exposure | Natural household exposure to an index patient with laboratory-confirmed (rapid-test) influenza, then household-level allocation to one of three arms. Quote: "control (lifestyle measures), control plus enhanced hand hygiene only, and control plus facemasks and enhanced hand hygiene"; randomized 134 / 136 / 137 households; interventions implemented at a home visit (target within 12 h, actual median delay much longer), contacts followed with daily symptom diaries and nasal+throat swabs on visits ~day 0, 3, and 6 |
| Comparator / control | Sham arm: **no** placebo and **yes** no-intervention cluster control. The third comparison arm is untouched normal household behavior. Quote from the interventions table text: the control group received "lifestyle education"; arms were "control (lifestyle measures), control plus enhanced hand hygiene only, and control plus facemasks and enhanced hand hygiene". Allocation was unblinded to participants, and the authors note contamination: "both interventions were practiced to some degree in the control group" |
| Endpoint | Named exactly: "The primary outcome measure was the secondary attack ratio at the individual level: the proportion of household contacts infected with influenza virus. We evaluated the secondary attack ratio by using a laboratory definition (a household contact with a nasal and throat swab specimen positive for influenza by RT-PCR) as the primary analysis and 2 clinical definitions of influenza based on self-reported data from the symptom diaries as secondary analyses" (definition 1: fever >=37.8 C plus >=2 of cough/headache/sore throat/myalgia; definition 2: fever >=37.8 C plus cough or sore throat). Secondary: household-level secondary attack ratio |
| Result | 60 of 794 contacts (8%) in 49 of 259 households (19%) had RT-PCR-confirmed influenza infection during follow-up; 150 (19%) and 41 (5%) met the two clinical definitions. Household-level secondary attack ratios 24% (control), 14% (hand hygiene), 18% (facemask+hand hygiene), P=0.37. Quote: "The risk of RT-PCR-confirmed influenza virus infection did not differ significantly between intervention groups." In the prespecified subgroup of 154 households where intervention started within 36 hours of index symptom onset, transmission was reduced, "attributable to fewer infections in the facemask plus hand hygiene group (adjusted odds ratio, 0.33 [95% CI 0.13 to 0.87])"; facemask+hygiene vs hand hygiene alone in the same subgroup: OR 0.72 (0.21-2.48), not significant. Risk was significantly higher for contacts aged 6-15 years |
| Main limitation | Stated by the authors: "The delay from index patient symptom onset to intervention and variable adherence may have mitigated intervention effectiveness" (median delay exceeded the target 12 h); "Only half of the index patients in the facemask plus hand hygiene group reported regular use of a surgical mask during follow-up"; contamination between groups; the 36-hour subgroup is post-randomization (timing of randomization relative to onset varied), so its significant OR is subgroup evidence, not the primary intention-to-treat result; no sham/placebo masking, so behavioral placebo effects are uncontrolled |

## Which gap and ladder rung this fills
- Queue GAP 2 (controlled natural-route transmission): this is the influenza anchor - actual households, actual index patients, laboratory-confirmed infection in contacts as the endpoint, randomized allocation at household level. It answers the question the challenge rung (Memoli 2015, Han 2021) cannot: whether intervention on a natural exposure route changes lab-confirmed transmission.
- The Herlocher 2004 ferret contact-transfer paper remains `unverifiable` in [[synthesis/source-verification-queue]]; this trial provides the human natural-route controlled comparison instead.
- Cross-cutting framework: supplies the natural-route intervention row alongside rung 7 (controlled human challenge) in [[synthesis/viral-disease-causation-evidence-ladder]]; new row in [[synthesis/influenza-influenza-virus-evidence-ladder]].

## What the experiment can answer
- That, in interpandemic-season Hong Kong households, hand-hygiene and facemask-plus-hand-hygiene interventions applied with real-world delay did not significantly reduce RT-PCR-confirmed or clinical influenza transmission compared with a no-intervention control group (per-protocol contamination and low adherence documented).
- That early application (within 36 h of index symptom onset) was associated with lower RT-PCR-confirmed transmission in the combination arm (aOR 0.33), with the caveats that this is a subgroup analysis and the trial was not powered primarily for it.
- That natural household transmission of influenza, under a named laboratory endpoint, occurs at measurable rates (~8% contact-level, ~19% household-level in the analyzed set) without any inoculation by the investigators.

## What it cannot answer
- Efficacy under high adherence or very early application as a randomized contrast; the 36-hour finding compares households whose timing was not randomized with respect to symptom onset within arm.
- Aerosol-vs-contact-vs-fomite route attribution; the interventions bundle (masks + soap + alcohol rub) cannot decompose transmission modes, as the authors state: "our study cannot precisely distinguish the relative contributions of the 2 interventions."
- Severity outcomes beyond mild illness, hospitalization, or pandemic-strain transmission (all index infections were interpandemic influenza A or B).
- Any claim about influenza causation itself; the trial presupposes the pathogen and measures transmission.

## Later inferential-leap risk
- The abstract conclusion states: "Hand hygiene and facemasks seemed to prevent household transmission of influenza virus when implemented within 36 hours of index patient symptom onset. These findings suggest that nonpharmaceutical interventions are important for mitigation of pandemic and interpandemic influenza." The first clause rests on the subgroup, not the null intention-to-treat result; the second extrapolates a household trial to pandemic-scale policy.
- The accompanying editors' note ("hand washing and facemasks seemed to prevent influenza transmission when healthy family members started using these measures within 36 hours") repeats the subgroup reading as the headline.
- Challenge-model or household attack rates in low-antibody or child-heavy households should not be read as population attack rates.

## Vault conclusion
Supported at the controlled natural-route rung for interpandemic influenza: under household-level randomization with an RT-PCR endpoint, the tested interventions did not significantly reduce transmission overall, with a significant subgroup-level reduction (aOR 0.33) only when initiated within 36 hours. The trial documents that natural household transmission with a named laboratory endpoint can be measured under randomized conditions - and that the measured effect of the interventions was not statistically significant as randomized. Natural-route evidence for influenza no longer rests only on the negative 1918-1919 challenge boundary ([[sources/rosenau-1919-experiments-to-determine-mode-of-spread-of-influenza]]).
