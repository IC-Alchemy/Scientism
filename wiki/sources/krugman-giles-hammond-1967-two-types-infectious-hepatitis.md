# Infectious Hepatitis Evidence for Two Distinctive Clinical Epidemiological and Immunological Types of Infection

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary human infection/challenge and contact-exposure paper distinguishing two hepatitis patterns before modern HAV/HBV naming
- Journal/year: *JAMA*, 1967
- Authors: Saul Krugman, Joan P. Giles, and Jack Hammond
- Source basis in hand: full-page scan of the original JAMA article obtained from the UK Infected Blood Inquiry evidence document, checked against the JAMA citation metadata

## Vault extraction
| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Study type | Seven Willowbrook human infection trials using oral serum exposure, intramuscular serum exposure, intimate contact exposure, and later rechallenge |
| Subject / sample | Newly admitted children at Willowbrook State School; serum pools/inocula included WSP-5, MS-1, and MS-2 derived from Willowbrook hepatitis cases |
| Exposure or intervention | WSP-5, MS-1, or MS-2 hepatitis-containing serum given orally or intramuscularly; some children served as uninoculated close-contact controls; later MS-1 rechallenge was used after prior MS-1 or MS-2 infection |
| Comparator / control | Unfed or uninoculated contact controls; comparison of MS-1-exposed and MS-2-exposed children; homologous MS-1 rechallenge versus heterologous MS-1 challenge after prior MS-2 infection |
| Endpoint | Hepatitis with jaundice, anicteric hepatitis, incubation period defined largely by SGOT rise above 100 units, duration of abnormal SGOT activity, thymol turbidity, contact spread, and immunity or lack of immunity on rechallenge |
| Result | MS-1 produced a shorter incubation pattern, brief abnormal SGOT activity, consistently abnormal thymol turbidity, high contact spread, and homologous immunity after rechallenge. MS-2 produced longer incubation, prolonged SGOT abnormality, frequently normal thymol turbidity, lower but present contact spread, oral infectivity in later trials, and no protection against later MS-1 challenge. |
| Main limitation | The study used a small, non-randomized, vulnerable institutional population in an ethically high-risk design. The inocula were hepatitis-containing sera, not purified viral preparations. The paper predates modern HBV markers, sequencing, culture, or direct virion identification, and endemic/contact conditions complicate clean attribution. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that two hepatitis-producing inoculum patterns were distinguishable in this Willowbrook setting by incubation, SGOT duration, thymol turbidity, contact spread, and rechallenge immunity.
- It can answer that the MS-2/serum-hepatitis-like pattern was infectious by mouth in the tested trials and could spread to some close contacts.
- It can answer that prior MS-2 infection did not protect children against later MS-1 challenge in the seventh trial.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot identify hepatitis B virus particles.
- It cannot prove purified-virus causation.
- It cannot sequence or culture a virus.
- It cannot establish the full modern HBV attribution chain by itself.
- It cannot generalize cleanly from Willowbrook institutional exposure conditions to all ordinary human transmission settings.

## Later inferential-leap risk
This paper is valuable because it separates hepatitis syndromes before modern viral naming. The leap begins if MS-2/serum-hepatitis-like disease is treated as already equivalent to fully identified hepatitis B virus causation. The paper supports syndrome and immunologic separation, not particle identity or molecular proof.

## What this wiki currently concludes
This source supports a narrow claim: in Krugman, Giles, and Hammond's Willowbrook series, two hepatitis-producing inoculum patterns were experimentally distinguished by clinical, biochemical, epidemiologic, and rechallenge-immunity endpoints. It does not itself identify HBV particles or complete the HBV causation ladder.
