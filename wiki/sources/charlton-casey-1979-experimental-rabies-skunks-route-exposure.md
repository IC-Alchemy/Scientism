# Experimental Rabies in Skunks Oral Nasal Tracheal and Intestinal Exposure

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary animal route-comparison paper for rabies exposure boundaries
- Journal/year: *Canadian Journal of Comparative Medicine*, 1979
- Authors: K. M. Charlton and G. A. Casey
- Source basis in hand: full-text PMC PDF

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Controlled route-comparison animal exposure study |
| Subject / sample | Male striped skunks exposed to CVS rabies virus prepared from infected mouse brain; surviving animals later challenged with street-rabies virus from skunk salivary glands |
| Exposure or intervention | Free-choice infected mouse brain, free-choice suspension, forced oral feeding with or without prior training, intratracheal instillation, intranasal instillation, and direct intestinal administration |
| Comparator / control | Route groups of five animals; no-dose controls in the later intramuscular street-virus challenge |
| Endpoint | Rabies illness/death, incubation period, fluorescent-antibody and mouse-inoculation confirmation, serum neutralizing antibody, and later intramuscular challenge survival |
| Result | All five intranasally exposed skunks developed rabies; two of five intratracheally exposed skunks and two of ten forced-fed skunks developed rabies. Free-choice oral exposure and direct intestinal exposure produced no rabies deaths. Most survivors later developed rabies after intramuscular street-virus challenge, and all five controls succumbed to that challenge. |
| Main limitation | This is a skunk route-boundary experiment with CVS virus and a later street-virus challenge. It does not test bite exposure directly and should not be generalized as a failure of parenteral rabies causation. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that intranasal exposure was much more efficient than the tested oral or intestinal routes in this skunk model.
- It can answer that oral exposure under these conditions gave inconsistent or absent disease depending on the protocol.
- It can answer that surviving exposure usually did not protect against later intramuscular challenge.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot refute bite-route rabies.
- It cannot define transmission in all carnivores.
- It cannot separate every possible oral mucosal exposure from accidental nasal exposure.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The leap begins if oral or intestinal negative results are retold as general rabies-causation failure. This paper is a route-boundary paper.

## Vault conclusion
Charlton and Casey 1979 supports a narrow route-boundary claim: in striped skunks, intranasal CVS exposure reliably produced rabies, while free-choice oral and intestinal exposures did not under the tested conditions.
