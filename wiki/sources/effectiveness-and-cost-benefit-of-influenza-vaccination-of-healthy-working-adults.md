# Effectiveness and Cost-Benefit of Influenza Vaccination of Healthy Working Adults

## TL;DR
This JAMA paper reports a double-blind randomized saline-placebo trial of trivalent inactivated influenza vaccine among healthy full-time working adults aged 18 to 64 across the 1997-1998 and 1998-1999 influenza seasons. It found strong season-dependent differences: in the mismatched 1997-1998 season, vaccine recipients had significantly more ILI-related sick days, lost workdays, and physician-visit work hours than placebo recipients, while in the matched 1998-1999 season vaccination reduced serologically confirmed influenza illness and several work-related clinical endpoints; vaccination did not produce net economic savings under the study's assumptions in either season.

- Primary trial report.
- Published in JAMA on 2000-10-04.

## Study design
- Study type: double-blind randomized placebo-controlled trial across two influenza seasons.
- Population: healthy full-time employees of Ford Motor Co. in Dearborn, Michigan, aged 18 to 64, without medical conditions for which influenza vaccination was then recommended.
- Sample size: 1184 randomized in 1997-1998 and 1191 randomized in 1998-1999.
- Intervention: trivalent inactivated influenza vaccine.
- Comparator: sterile saline injection, prepared in identical syringes.
- Follow-up: twice-monthly electronic surveys from November through March, plus adverse-effect surveys after injection.
- Laboratory subset: serology was collected in about 23 percent of participants each year.

## Endpoints and results
| Question | Endpoint | Result | What it can answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Did vaccination reduce laboratory-confirmed influenza illness? | ILI with serologic evidence of influenza infection in the tested subset | 1997-1998: vaccine efficacy 50%, P=.33; 1998-1999: vaccine efficacy 86%, P=.001 | The trial can answer season-specific efficacy against the study's laboratory-confirmed influenza-illness definition in the serology subset. |
| Did vaccination reduce clinical ILI and related utilization? | Self-reported ILI, ILI-related sick days, physician visits, physician-visit work hours, and work absenteeism | 1997-1998: vaccine recipients reported significantly more ILI-related sick days, lost workdays, and lost work hours for physician visits than placebo recipients; vaccine effectiveness was -10% against ILI, P=.45, and -3% against URI, P=.75. 1998-1999: ILI reduced 34%, physician visits 42%, lost workdays 32% | The trial can answer whether vaccination changed these named short-term clinical and work-utilization endpoints in this workplace population. |
| Did vaccination save money? | Societal and health care payer cost of ILI plus vaccination cost | Societal net cost: $65.59 per vaccinated person in 1997-1998 and $11.17 per vaccinated person in 1998-1999; payer perspective did not save money in either year | The trial can answer cost-benefit under the study's wage, utilization, vaccine-cost, and ILI-rate assumptions. |
| Did vaccination cause common short-term adverse effects? | Self-reported adverse effects in the first 7 days | Arm soreness and injection-site redness were more common in vaccine recipients; other listed symptoms were not significantly more common | The trial can answer common short-term reactogenicity in this sample; it cannot answer rare or long-term safety outcomes. |

## What the source claims
- Influenza vaccination of healthy working adults younger than 65 can reduce ILI, lost workdays, and physician visits during years when vaccine strains and circulating viruses are similar.
- In the mismatched 1997-1998 season, vaccine recipients had significantly worse ILI-related utilization endpoints than placebo recipients, even though the serology subset did not show a statistically significant increase in laboratory-confirmed influenza.
- The same intervention may not provide overall economic benefit in most years.
- Laboratory confirmation matters because clinical influenza definitions can be nonspecific when other respiratory pathogens circulate.

## What this wiki currently concludes
- This is a useful direct-study anchor for [[concepts/clinical-endpoints]], [[concepts/placebo-controls]], and [[comparisons/disease-prevention-vs-health-outcomes]] because the source names the intervention, comparator, endpoint, and result.
- The trial supports narrower, season-specific claims about inactivated influenza vaccine effects in healthy working adults, especially when vaccine and circulating strains match.
- The trial also preserves a negative season-specific clinical-utilization result: during the mismatched 1997-1998 season, random assignment to the vaccine group was associated with significantly more ILI-related sick days, lost workdays, and physician-visit work hours than assignment to the saline-placebo group. Within this trial design, that is direct randomized evidence of harm on those named endpoints, though not proof of rare serious adverse events or long-term harm.
- It does not support a broad claim that influenza vaccination improves overall health in all populations or all seasons.
- It does not answer pediatric vaccine questions, elderly-risk questions, all-cause mortality, rare adverse events, or long-term net health outcomes.

## Warning gap / ethical significance
The paper's abstract conclusion emphasizes that vaccination can reduce ILI, lost workdays, and physician visits when vaccine and circulating viruses are similar, and that it may not provide economic benefit in most years. That conclusion does not foreground the unfavorable 1997-1998 clinical-utilization finding in the vaccine group.

For this vault, that omission is highly relevant to [[concepts/scientism]]: a randomized trial can contain a direct harm signal on clinically meaningful endpoints while the author-level conclusion frames the result mainly around conditional benefit and cost-benefit. The experiment shows that, in this population and season, the vaccine group had worse ILI-related sick-day and work-loss outcomes. A broader claim that all influenza vaccines cause serious harm would require additional direct studies, but this trial should not be summarized as merely showing "no reduction" in 1997-1998.

## Limitations
- Main limitation: the result is specific to this study population, two influenza seasons, and the named clinical, utilization, serology, and cost endpoints.
- Single employer and healthy working-adult population limits external validity.
- Only two influenza seasons were observed, and the two seasons differed by vaccine-strain match.
- Laboratory-confirmed influenza illness was assessed only in a subset of participants.
- ILI, physician visits, medication use, and lost workdays were reported through surveys.
- Economic conclusions depend on assumed wage rates, vaccination costs, ILI attack rates, and the study's human-capital cost model.
- The trial was not designed to detect rare adverse events, long-term safety outcomes, mortality, hospitalization benefit, or broader overall-health effects.
