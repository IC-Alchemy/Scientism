# Experiments to Determine Mode of Spread of Influenza

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary influenza transmission experiment report
- Journal/date: *Journal of the American Medical Association*, 1919-08-02
- Author: Milton J. Rosenau
- Citation details: volume 73, issue 5, page 311
- Source basis in hand: extracted primary text from the 1919 JAMA report in `tmp/rosenau-1919-jama.txt`

> Warning: This page is based on local extracted primary text from the 1919 report, not on a modern abstract. The extraction is sufficient for the main experiment descriptions and Rosenau's closing caution, but a full scanned-page check would still be useful for exact denominators and any wording outside the extracted text.

## Vault extraction
| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Study type | Human volunteer transmission experiments during the 1918-1919 influenza outbreak |
| Sample / subjects | Navy volunteers, mostly ages 18 to 25, described as in good physical condition; Rosenau says 100 volunteers were used overall, with individual experiments using smaller groups |
| Exposure or intervention | Multiple attempted transmission methods: Pfeiffer bacillus cultures, mixed respiratory secretions, filtered respiratory material, direct nose-to-nose and throat-to-throat transfer, citrated whole blood, filtered upper-respiratory material injected subcutaneously, and close-contact ward exposure |
| Comparator or control | No formal randomized control group is described in the extracted text; the paper instead compares several exposure methods and notes that some volunteers with prior typical influenza were selected for immunity or control questions |
| Endpoint | Whether volunteers developed recognizable illness after exposure, monitored by isolation, repeated temperature checks, and clinical observation |
| Result | Across the specifically described exposure series in the extracted text, Rosenau reports no volunteers "took sick" in any way under the tested conditions |
| Main limitation | The report describes negative results in specific challenge conditions but Rosenau explicitly warns against broad positive conclusions from those negative results and notes uncertainty about susceptibility, immunity, diagnosis, and unknown transmission factors |

## What the paper can answer
- It can answer that Rosenau reported repeated negative human transmission attempts under several named exposure methods during the outbreak.
- It can answer that these were not merely theoretical failures: the report describes direct secretion transfer, filtered material, blood injection, and close face-to-face ward exposure with coughing and breathing.
- It can answer that Rosenau himself did not treat the negative series as proof that influenza was noninfectious.
- It can answer that Rosenau cited similar negative Goat Island results in volunteers thought, so far as known, not to have been exposed, as part of the unresolved transmission problem.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot justify a vault claim that influenza is noninfectious.
- It cannot establish that no transmissible causal agent exists for influenza.
- It cannot settle the whole influenza causation literature, because this is one outbreak-era volunteer series with the diagnostic and methodological limits Rosenau himself describes.
- It cannot distinguish, by itself, whether the negative results were best explained by donor selection, timing, prior immunity, case misclassification, route mismatch, dose mismatch, or other unknown factors.

## Extracted primary text status
This source page is based on extracted primary text rather than a modern abstract. The extracted text preserves the main Gallops Island experiments, the ward-exposure procedure, the negative-result counts for the named groups, Rosenau's closing caution, and the note about McCoy's Goat Island series.

For this vault, that is enough to move the influenza entry from commentary-led to primary-report-led, while still keeping the interpretive boundary narrow.

## Key negative-result experiments preserved in the extracted text
- Nineteen volunteers received a large mixture of thirteen Pfeiffer bacillus strains sprayed into the nose, eyes, and throat while inspiring; Rosenau reports that none took sick.
- Ten volunteers received mixed mouth, nose, throat, and bronchial material from influenza cases sprayed into the nostrils, throat, and eye; none took sick.
- Filtered material from the same sort of respiratory source was instilled into other volunteers and also produced no results.
- Another ten volunteers received 6 c.c. of similar mixed material after the collection-to-exposure interval was shortened to one hour and forty minutes; none took sick.
- Nineteen volunteers received material transferred directly from donors' noses and throats, including some donors in the first day of disease; none took sick in any way.
- Ten volunteers received 10 c.c. each of citrated whole blood pooled from five febrile influenza cases; none took sick in any way.
- Ten volunteers received 3.5 c.c. each of filtered upper-respiratory material by subcutaneous injection; none took sick in any way.
- Ten volunteers had close ward exposure with ten influenza patients each, including face-to-face breathing and direct coughing at close range; none took sick in any way.

## Rosenau's own caution
The report's conclusion is methodologically important for this vault because Rosenau resists overreading the negative findings. He says investigators must be careful not to draw positive conclusions from negative results of this kind, then offers several possible reasons for failure, including lack of susceptibility, prior immunity, diagnostic uncertainty, and unknown transmission factors.

The extracted text also states that Dr. McCoy and Dr. Richey carried out a similar series on Goat Island, San Francisco, using volunteers thought not to have been exposed to the outbreak, and that they also had negative results.

Rosenau closes by saying that perhaps the team learned that they were "not quite sure what we know about the disease." In this vault, that matters because the primary report itself frames the experiments as a boundary on confidence, not as a final refutation of infectious spread.

## What this wiki currently concludes
- This source directly supports a narrow claim that repeated human challenge and close-contact procedures described by Rosenau failed to produce recognizable influenza illness in the reported volunteer groups under those tested conditions.
- It supports a narrower methodological warning that early influenza transmission knowledge was less secure than later simplified retellings can imply.
- It does not support a vault-level conclusion that influenza is noninfectious.
- It also does not support a vault-level conclusion that the negative series was meaningless; the primary report is important precisely because the failures were repeated, varied, and acknowledged by Rosenau as perplexing.
