# Experimental Infection of Tree Shrews with Coxsackievirus A16

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary animal-model paper for Coxsackievirus A16 systemic infection and tissue distribution
- Journal/year: *Zoological Research*, 2014
- Authors: J. P. Li, Y. Liao, Y. Zhang, J. J. Wang, L. C. Wang, K. Feng, Q. H. Li, and L. D. Liu
- Source basis in hand: full-text PMC article

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Experimental animal infection model |
| Subject / sample | 12 infected tree shrews and 3 mock-infected controls |
| Exposure or intervention | Nasal-spray inoculation with Coxsackievirus A16 GX18 strain isolated from a child with mild disease |
| Comparator / control | Saline mock-infected tree shrews |
| Endpoint | Fever and inflammatory changes, viral RNA distribution, viral antigen in tissues, histopathology, and fecal shedding |
| Result | Infected animals showed fever/inflammatory markers. Viral RNA or antigen was detected in multiple tissues including lung, spleen, heart, muscle, and CNS, and fecal shedding was observed. Typical human HFMD vesicles were not seen. |
| Main limitation | Small animal study with artificial route and dose; limited reproduction of human skin and oral lesions. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that the GX18 Coxsackievirus A16 strain established systemic infection markers in tree shrews under nasal-spray inoculation.
- It can answer that viral RNA/antigen distribution and fecal shedding were detectable in this model.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot prove natural human transmission probability.
- It cannot directly reproduce human lesion tropism or ordinary HFMD clinical disease.
- It cannot define pediatric severity or causation in naturally exposed children.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The risk is treating tree-shrew systemic spread as direct proof of the same pathogenesis in children. This paper is an animal-model rung and should remain separate from human lesion isolation and serology.

## Vault conclusion
Li et al. 2014 supports a Coxsackie A16 animal-model rung with mock controls and tissue-distribution endpoints. It does not settle ordinary human HFMD causation or lesion pathogenesis.
