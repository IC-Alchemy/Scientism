# Schmidt et al. 1986 - Seattle families HCoV-229E/OC43 antibody rises

> [!note] Note
> Full text read and verified 2026-11-10 (loop run 16, queue item 29) from the complete 7-page scanned PDF (PMC OA deposit, PMC7110132) recovered via the Internet Archive captures of the PMC PDF URL; the live PMC PDF route served a reCAPTCHA and Europe PMC's PMCID metadata was inconsistent across records (its core record pointed at PMC264068, a different Schmidt paper). This paper is from the same Seattle Virus Watch VI program and the same families as the influenza papers that remain full-text unreachable in this vault ([[sources/kim-2012-kenya-household-pdm09-transmission-cohort]] holds the influenza cohort rung; queue items 6/7/27 document the unreachable Fox/Hall AJE influenza papers).

## What experiment was run

- **Study type:** prospective family cohort (observational; no comparator arm - recorded explicitly). Ten families were selected from some 200 Seattle families entering continuing observation for influenzavirus infections during 1975-1979 (Seattle Virus Watch VI, design paper Fox et al. AJE 1982;116:212-227), each family required to have at least one preschool child at entry in fall 1975.
- **Subjects:** 44 children and adults (at entry: nine aged 0-4 years, eleven 5-9, four 10-14, twenty adults); 128 person-years of observation.
- **Exposure:** living in the observed families under the program's continuous surveillance; nose-throat specimens for culture collected biweekly and when illness was observed; blood specimens collected three times yearly (late fall October/November, spring March/April, late summer July/August - approximately every four months, an interval the authors note was designed to bracket influenza seasons, not the conventional seasons). 419 blood specimens examined.
- **Comparator/control:** none. This is a natural cohort, not a controlled experiment; no sham or placebo element exists. Recorded as such.
- **Endpoint (named exactly):** "significant antibody rise" to 229E or OC43 whole-virus antigen, defined as an ELISA ratio >=1.8 (maximum/minimum optical density between sequential serum specimens at the same dilution; titers = reciprocal of the highest dilution giving OD 0.35). The 1.8 cutoff was chosen for its correlation with fourfold-or-greater virus-neutralizing and complement-fixing rises in paired sera from 200 unrelated respiratory patients (unpublished validation).
- **Infection-marker character of the endpoint:** for most observations the endpoint is serology, not virus detection. Only one virus-detection datum appears: OC43 antigen was detected by ELISA in nose-throat specimens from one of the two primary-infection children (unpublished data stated in the discussion).

## Result

- **Prevalence at baseline:** all but two children under two years old already had antibody to 229E (one also lacked OC43); geometric mean titers rose with age and were higher for OC43 than 229E in every age group.
- **Infection frequency:** 92 significant rises over 419 sera / 128 person-years - 56 to OC43, 36 to 229E - i.e. 0.7 rises per person-year overall; by age (Table 2): 0-4 y combined ~1.0/person-year (0.44 229E + 0.55 OC43), 5-9 y ~0.9, 10-14 y ~0.8, adults 0.39. "The frequency of significant antibody rises in children, about one per person-year, was almost three times higher than in adults."
- **Timing:** rises occurred in every sampling interval; OC43 rises peaked in the winter interval (October/November -> March/April); 229E showed no seasonal pattern.
- **Household clustering:** concurrent significant OC43 rises in >=2 members of the same family in 15 instances, 229E in 7, and mixed OC43/229E across members in 8 - the authors take this as the two viruses circulating "simultaneously as well as independently."
- **Reinfection:** repeated significant rises to the same virus (21 OC43, 8 229E) were "always separated by at least eight months"; two successive rises in one person occurred 14 times, each time to different viruses; simultaneous rises to both viruses in one person occurred in 3 children (not significantly above the 5 expected by chance).
- **Primary infection:** seroconversion was observed in only two children, both under two at entry (family C: OC43 August->November 1976, then 229E November 1976->March 1977; family D: pre-existing OC43, then 229E seroconversion November 1976->March 1977). Both children had reported illness episodes.
- **Illness linkage - explicitly not possible:** "Because of the four-month intervals between collection of blood specimens, it was not possible to relate specific illnesses to observed rises in antibody titers." What could be said: 36% of significant rises in adults and 40% in children occurred during intervals in which no respiratory or gastrointestinal illness was reported; i.e. roughly 40% of all serologic rises had no temporally reported illness.

## Main limitations (including the authors' own)

- The four-month bleed interval destroys illness-to-rise temporal resolution; ~40% of rises without reported illness may include illnesses reported outside the bracketing interval.
- The endpoint is an antibody rise, not virus isolation; the authors' own boundary: "We have presumed that these rises reflected reinfections with viruses related to one or the other prototype but it remains possible that at least some reflect chronic infections accompanied by antibody rises when host defenses weakened."
- The conservative 1.8 cutoff probably undercounts infections ("it is probable that the rates reported in this study underestimate the true frequency of infections"); ratios 1.5-1.8 often correlated with fourfold VN rises and 18/32 occurred in family clusters.
- Ten families / 44 persons deliberately selected for preschool children - a small, age-biased subsample of the ~200-family program.
- OC43 seasonality is inferred from sampling intervals built to bracket influenza seasons (authors flag the mismatch with conventional seasons).

## Which gap and ladder rung this fills

- **GAP 3 (prospective cohort with exposure + infection markers + timing in the same people), first row for seasonal human coronaviruses, in its serologic form.** It supplies: prospective serial sampling of the same persons, a named infection marker (ELISA ratio >=1.8), interval timing, and family-clustering structure. It does **not** supply a disease-timing endpoint - the paper says so itself - and therefore fills only part of the GAP 3 template. Recorded in [[synthesis/viral-disease-causation-evidence-ladder]] (GAP 3 note, fourth pathogen group).
- For the vault's themes this row is useful precisely because of the boundary it draws: frequent reinfection (0.7/person-year) with roughly 40% of infection-marker events unaccompanied by reported illness - infection markers and disease are separable events in the same people, measured in one dataset.
- Same-family program note: the Seattle Virus Watch families also generated the influenza papers in queue items 6/7/27, which remain full-text unreachable; this OA AJE deposit is the program's reachable window.

## Inferential leaps beyond the experiment

1. **Antibody rise = infection (usually reinfection).** The paper presumes rises reflect reinfection with viruses "related to one or the other prototype"; it cannot exclude antigenically related strains or chronic infection reactivating antibody rises (authors' own caveat). The leap is from a titer change to an infection event.
2. **Short-term homologous immunity.** "These results suggest that infection with one virus may convey short-term immunity to homologous reinfection" - inferred from the >=8-month spacing of same-virus rises; no person-time-at-risk analysis is presented.
3. **Seasonality of OC43** from a sampling frame designed for influenza-season bracketing; interval placement is not calendar seasonality.
