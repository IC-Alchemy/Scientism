# Sequence-Based Identification of Microbial Pathogens: A Reconsideration of Koch's Postulates

## TL;DR
Fredericks and Relman is useful for this vault because it gives a molecular-era caution: nucleic-acid sequences can be powerful disease leads, but their evidentiary value depends on localization, regularity, controls, biological plausibility, and reduction or disappearance with disease resolution.

## Source type and scope
- Methodological review in *Clinical Microbiology Reviews*.
- Lens lookup: Lens `075-096-104-194-867`; DOI `10.1128/cmr.9.1.18-33.1996`; PMID `8665474`; PMCID `PMC172879`.
- It is not a primary experiment for one disease; it is a framework source for evaluating molecular evidence.

## What the source claims
- Classical Koch postulates do not handle many modern pathogen-discovery cases cleanly.
- Sequence detection can strengthen causal inference when the sequence is repeatedly found in diseased hosts, localized to diseased tissue, less common or absent in controls, biologically coherent, and decreases with disease resolution or effective intervention.
- A mere sequence hit is not enough to settle causation.

## What experiment or observation is involved
- No single intervention or exposure experiment is being tested.
- The relevant observation class is pathogen-associated nucleic-acid detection, interpreted through proposed criteria rather than through one trial endpoint.

## What question the paper is capable of answering
`How should nucleic-acid sequence evidence be evaluated when asking whether a microbe is causally related to disease?`

It cannot answer whether any specific virus causes a named disease unless paired with disease-specific evidence.

## What this wiki currently concludes
- This paper supports keeping PCR, RT-qPCR, sequencing, and metagenomic findings in their own evidence bucket.
- For this vault, molecular detection should not be upgraded into disease causation unless the source also names the disease endpoint, controls, tissue localization or timing, and a plausible bridge to pathogenesis.
