# Risk Factors Associated With Secondary Measles Transmission in Index Cases

## Source type
- Role in this vault: case-ascertained natural-exposure transmission analysis tying named exposure (close contact with a laboratory-confirmed measles index case during a defined infectious period), infection markers (contact PCR or IgM confirmation), and timing (21-day contact monitoring from last exposure) in the same population; fills GAP 3 (exposure + infection markers + disease timing) in [[synthesis/source-verification-queue]] for measles, whose ladder previously had no natural-human-transmission row at all.
- Journal/year: *Journal of Epidemiology*, 2026;36(10):307-312 (received 20 June 2025, accepted 14 January 2026); CC BY open access
- Authors: Takahashi Yuki, Tanaka Hideo, Shibata Toshiyuki, Tanikake Chisato, Moriwaki Takashi, Miyazono Masaya, Matsumoto Sayuri, Kinoshita Masaru, Nagai Hitomi, Shirai Chika, Takayama Yoshihiro, Okada Megumi, Tsuda Yuko, Hazama Ayako, Ohara Toshitake, Yoshida Hideki, Kurata Takako, Kanbayashi Daiki, Taniguchi Chie, Motomura Kazushi (Osaka Prefectural Government public health centers, Osaka Institute of Public Health, Aichi Medical University)
- Funding: grant-in-aid "Public Health Research on Emerging Infectious Diseases in Comprehensive Programs on Community Health", Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, Japan; conflicts declared none
- Ethics: Ethics Committee of the Osaka Institute of Public Health (approval No. 1912-06), conducted under the Infectious Diseases Control Law
- Source basis in hand: complete open-access full text from Europe PMC (PMC13562612), including Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion, Tables 1-2, and the reference list

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Retrospective analytical epidemiological study of surveillance data from the Osaka Prefecture measles outbreak (population 8.8 million), November 2018-March 2019; index cases and their close contacts as recorded by routine public-health-center investigations; not randomized, not a serial-sampling cohort |
| Subject / sample | 105 index cases (defined as "the first family member referring to the hospital..." - in practice, primary reported cases; contacts with links to other domestic cases excluded) and their 9,846 close contacts (average 93.7 per index); 72 secondary cases. Index case definition: symptoms suggestive of measles plus PCR or IgM confirmation; N-450 genotyping per WHO available in the surveillance system. Classical measles = all three of generalized rash, fever >38.5 °C, and cough/coryza/conjunctivitis; modified measles = one or two of the three |
| Exposure | Close contact with the index case, defined as "anyone who had direct contact with a measles patient during the infectious period (from 1 day before the onset of measles symptoms to 3 days after the fever subsides), anyone who may have inhaled droplets from the patient's cough, sneeze, or conversation within a 2-meter radius, or anyone who shared an enclosed space (including spaces with air conditioning) with the index cases"; contacts monitored for 21 days from last contact |
| Comparator / control | Sham arm: **no** sham-exposure procedure exists in a natural transmission study. The contrast is between index-case strata (unvaccinated vs 1 vs >=2 vaccine doses; classical vs modified measles), adjusted for sex, age, number of contacts, vaccination status, and symptoms. Vaccination histories of index cases were verified against mother-and-child health handbooks or official certificates where possible, with recollection as fallback |
| Endpoint | Named exactly - secondary cases were "close contacts of index cases who developed symptoms consistent with measles and were laboratory-confirmed by PCR or IgM antibody testing"; the secondary attack rate (SAR) is "the number of secondary cases divided by the total number of close contacts of each index case" |
| Result | Overall SAR 0.73% (72/9,846; 95% CI 0.56-0.90). By index vaccination status: 0 doses SAR 1.19% (60/5,035), 1 dose 0.20% (4/2,019), >=2 doses 0.13% (3/2,283), unknown 0.98% (5/509); trend P<0.01; adjusted OR 0.22 (0.08-0.65) for one dose and 0.25 (0.07-0.85) for >=2 doses vs zero doses. By clinical form: classical 0.93% (69/7,414) vs modified 0.12% (3/2,432), aOR 0.12 (0.03-0.39). Male index cases 1.14% (52/4,566) vs female 0.38% (20/5,280), aOR 0.42 (0.24-0.73) for female. IgM-positive index 1.24% (61/4,922) vs IgM-negative 0.00% (0/748). Contact-count gradient: 1-9 contacts SAR 6.67%, 10-99 1.30% (aOR 0.10), >=100 0.50% (aOR 0.04), interpreted by the authors as household-type contacts dominating the small-number stratum. Index age 15-24 highest infectivity (aOR 0.32 for <=14 and 0.37 for >=40 vs 15-24) |
| Main limitation | Stated by the authors: (1) "the measles vaccination history of the close contacts was not available", and because residence/religion/economics cluster in families and friendships, "these factors might overestimate the risk of causing secondary infections from index cases without vaccination history"; (2) the three classic symptoms were "recorded as a single combined variable", so cough could not be assessed separately; (3) environmental factors beyond the patient were not considered - large-contact-number settings "may have occurred in more spacious environments, such as mass gatherings, where the risk of transmission might have been lower compared to household settings" |

## What the experiment can answer
- That natural contact exposure to laboratory-confirmed measles cases produced laboratory-confirmed secondary infections in monitored contacts at measurable, stratum-specific rates (0.73% overall; 1.19% from unvaccinated index cases) in an elimination-setting outbreak.
- That, in these surveillance data, index-case vaccination count and clinical form (classical vs modified) were each independently associated with onward transmission (aOR 0.22-0.25 and 0.12), with the vaccination gradient holding after adjustment for sex, age, contact count, and symptoms.
- That transmission potential varied by index age (highest 15-24) and by contact setting density (small contact numbers = higher SAR).

## What it cannot answer
- Asymptomatic contact infection: the secondary-case definition requires symptoms plus PCR/IgM, so subclinical contact infections are invisible by construction; the rung cannot speak to total (symptomatic + silent) transmission.
- Confounded exposure contrast: contact-side vaccination histories were not collected, so household/friend similarity could make the unvaccinated-index stratum look more transmissible than its biology alone; the authors state this direction of bias explicitly.
- Cough-specific mechanism: symptoms were one combined variable; the cough-intensity mechanism discussed in the paper comes from cited studies, not from these data.
- Absolute infectiousness per contact-minute: exposure intensity is not measured - "close contact" is a category, not a dose; the enclosed-space clause mixes household, school, workplace, and transit exposure.
- IgM-timing interpretation: the 0.00% SAR for IgM-negative index cases may partly reflect testing-date artifacts (IgM not yet detectable early) and faster isolation, which the authors themselves note could not be disentangled.

## Inferential leaps to watch
- From association to policy: "The indirect protective effect of measles vaccination would present important evidence supporting the further strengthening of vaccination policies" - indirect (community) protection was not measured on the contact side; only index-side vaccination strata were observed.
- From lower SAR in vaccinated index cases to mechanism: "This suggests that the suppression of viral replication, likely due to immunological mechanisms, explains the association" - shedding was not measured here; the mechanism is imported from cited studies (lower viral load in breakthrough infections).
- From modified-measles SAR to the vaccinated-case question generally: modified measles occurs "not only in vaccinated measles cases but also in pediatric patients still with maternal antibodies" (authors' own citation), so the symptom gradient is not a pure vaccine-effect gradient.
