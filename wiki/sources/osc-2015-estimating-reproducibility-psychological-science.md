# Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary direct-replication project; the strongest inspectable anchor currently in hand for the replication-crisis concept
- Journal/year: *Science*, 2015
- Authors: Open Science Collaboration (270 named co-authors)
- Source basis in hand: PubMed abstract record; the paper's method, design counts, and result statistics are stated in the abstract itself

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Multi-lab systematic direct-replication project of published studies, with predefined materials, analysis plans, and replications run to high statistical power |
| Subject / sample | 100 experimental and correlational studies sampled from three psychology journals (2008 volumes); 97 of the 100 received completed replications by independent teams |
| Intervention or exposure | Re-running each original study's experimental or correlational design using original materials when available, at multiple independent laboratories |
| Comparator / control | Each replication is compared against its own original published result (effect size, significance, subjective similarity) |
| Endpoint | Statistical significance of the replication (p < 0.05), ratio of replication to original effect size, whether the original effect size falls inside the replication's 95% confidence interval, and blinded subjective rating of replication similarity |
| Result | Replication effects were half the magnitude of original effects on average. 97% of original studies had statistically significant results; 36% of replications did. 47% of original effect sizes were inside the replication's 95% CI; 39% of replications were subjectively rated to have replicated. Pooling original and replication data left 68% statistically significant assuming no bias in the originals. Replication success tracked the strength of the original evidence more than team characteristics. |
| Main limitation | A failed replication is not proof the original claim is false — sampling error, population differences, and material fidelity all cut both ways. The sample is one field (psychology), one publication year (2008), three journals. Correlational studies cannot be re-run identically. Subjective replication ratings introduce judgment. |

## What the experiment can answer
- What fraction of a defined, dated sample of psychology findings produced significant results when their published methods were re-run by independent teams with original materials: 36%.
- Whether original effect sizes shrank on average under independent replication: they halved.
- Whether the originals' statistical evidence was unusually strong relative to what independent re-runs produce: it was — 97% of originals were significant, which is itself a signal of selective reporting in the original literature.

## What it cannot answer
- Whether any specific individual claim outside the sampled 100 is false.
- Whether replication failure indicates fraud, error, bias, or true effect-size heterogeneity — the design cannot separate these.
- Anything about fields other than psychology; the design is field-specific and date-specific.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The overreach runs in both directions. Treating "36% replicated" as "64% of psychology is false" ignores the confidence-interval and pooled-analysis structure of the design. Conversely, treating the 68% pooled-significance figure as vindication ignores that pooling assumes unbiased originals, which the design itself flags as doubtful.

## Vault conclusion
This source supports a narrow claim: when 100 psychology studies' methods were re-run by independent teams, replication effect sizes were half the originals and 36% of replications were statistically significant, while 97% of the originals had been. The project is the vault's current anchor for what a direct replication program looks like and what it can and cannot establish.
