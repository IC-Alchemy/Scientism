# The Infectious Myth Busted Part 1: The Rosenau Spanish Flu Experiments (1918)

## Source metadata

- Type: commentary / blog post
- Tier: Tier 3
- Author: Mike Stone
- Published: 2021-10-03
- URL: <https://cms.viroliegy.com/2021/10/03/the-infectious-myth-busted-part-1-the-rosenau-spanish-flu-experiments-1918/>

> Warning: This page summarizes a Tier 3 commentary source. It can surface research leads and disputed interpretations, but it does not by itself justify factual conclusions for the wiki.

## What the source claims

The article argues that the Rosenau influenza transmission experiments in 1918, along with related follow-up work, "proved that the flu is not infectious at all." It presents repeated failed transmission attempts as evidence against ordinary person-to-person infectious spread and extends that argument into a broader rejection of virology's account of viral disease transmission.

The article also claims that:

- multiple experimental exposure methods failed to produce influenza in volunteers
- these negative results materially undermine mainstream accounts of the 1918 influenza pandemic
- later virology relied on artificial laboratory methods rather than demonstrating natural human-to-human transmission
- historical failure to transmit influenza experimentally is evidence against the broader idea of viral infectiousness

## What experiment or evidence the source discusses

The article quotes at length from Rosenau's report on human transmission experiments during the 1918 influenza pandemic and references a later historical review discussing Gallops Island and Angel Island work. The discussed procedures include:

- exposure to cultures associated with influenza, including Pfeiffer's bacillus
- transfer of respiratory secretions from influenza patients to volunteers
- direct nose-to-nose and throat-to-throat transfer attempts
- blood injection and filtered material injection
- close-contact exposure in hospital wards, including face-to-face breathing and coughing

The source emphasizes that these attempts reportedly did not produce influenza in the volunteers under the described conditions.

## What question the experiments were capable of answering

Based on the material quoted in the article, the Rosenau experiments were capable of testing a narrower question:

- whether the specific sampled materials, donors, timing, case selection, and exposure methods used in those experiments produced recognizable influenza illness in the enrolled volunteers

They were not, on their own, capable of resolving every broader question the article attaches to them, such as:

- whether influenza is infectious in all circumstances
- whether influenza had no transmissible causal agent
- whether later virology is generally invalid
- whether negative human challenge results overturn the broader epidemiology of influenza-like illness

## What the evidence level currently supports

From this source alone, the evidence level supports only a limited and careful statement:

- the article reports that Rosenau and related investigators obtained negative or inconclusive transmission results in the specific experiments it summarizes

The source itself also includes a caution from Rosenau against drawing strong positive conclusions from those negative results. As presented here, the experiments appear to raise methodological and interpretive questions about case definition, donor selection, timing, exposure route, susceptibility, and unknown transmission factors. They do not, by themselves, justify the article's broader conclusion that influenza is "not infectious at all."

Because this is a Tier 3 commentary source, any stronger conclusion would require direct review of the primary reports and relevant historical scholarship.

## Inferential leaps made by the source

The article moves from a narrow set of negative experimental results to broader claims that exceed what those experiments alone can establish. The main inferential leaps are:

- from failed transmission in specific human challenge conditions to a general claim of non-infectiousness
- from uncertainty about causation and transmission mechanisms in 1918 to rejection of later virological frameworks
- from criticism of particular experimental methods to a broader claim that viral infectious disease is a myth

## What remains unresolved

This source leaves several key questions unresolved:

- how the original Rosenau report framed its own limits, methods, and interpretation in full context
- how later historians of influenza interpret these experiments
- whether the negative results are best explained by failed sampling, timing, immunity, case misclassification, route of exposure, or some other factor
- how the Angel Island and related follow-up experiments compare methodologically to the Gallops Island work
- whether the article's broader anti-infectiousness conclusions are supported by primary literature rather than commentary

## Preliminary wiki conclusion

This source is best treated as a research lead and interpretive argument, not as a stand-alone evidentiary basis for conclusions about influenza transmission. It is relevant to pages on disputed interpretations of early influenza experiments, methodological limits of transmission studies, and inferential overreach from negative experimental findings.
