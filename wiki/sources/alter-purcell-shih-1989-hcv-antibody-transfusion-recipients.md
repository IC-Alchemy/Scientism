# Detection of Antibody to Hepatitis C Virus in Prospectively Followed Transfusion Recipients

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary prospective transfusion-recipient serology and donor-linkage paper for HCV
- Journal/year: *New England Journal of Medicine*, 1989
- Authors: Harvey J. Alter, Robert H. Purcell, James W. Shih, Jacqueline C. Melpolder, Michael Houghton, Qui-Lim Choo, and George Kuo
- Source basis in hand: full-text NEJM scan obtained from the UK Infected Blood Inquiry evidence document, checked against DOI and PubMed metadata

## Vault extraction
| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Study type | Prospective transfusion-recipient follow-up using stored coded donor and recipient sera, anti-HCV radioimmunoassay, ALT monitoring, anti-HBc and donor ALT surrogate assays, and biopsy confirmation for chronic cases |
| Subject / sample | Twenty selected open-heart-surgery transfusion recipients from earlier NIH prospective studies: 15 well-characterized chronic transfusion-associated non-A non-B hepatitis cases and 5 acute resolving cases; linked donor samples when available |
| Exposure or intervention | Blood transfusion exposure followed by serial post-transfusion sampling; anti-HCV testing used recombinant C100-3 antigen from the HCV cloning work |
| Comparator / control | Pre-transfusion recipient samples; donor-recipient linkage; comparison of chronic versus resolving cases; comparison of anti-HCV with donor ALT and anti-HBc surrogate markers |
| Endpoint | Anti-HCV seroconversion, timing after transfusion and after hepatitis onset, persistence or disappearance of antibody, donor anti-HCV status, ALT-defined hepatitis, biopsy-confirmed chronic hepatitis, and surrogate-marker detection |
| Result | All 15 chronic biopsy-supported non-A non-B hepatitis recipients seroconverted to anti-HCV. Three of five acute resolving cases seroconverted. Mean delay to anti-HCV seroconversion, excluding one late case, was 21.9 weeks after transfusion and 15 weeks after clinical hepatitis onset. Anti-HCV-positive donors were found in 14 of 16 fully evaluable anti-HCV-positive recipient cases. Only about half of anti-HCV-positive donors would have been detected by ALT/anti-HBc surrogate assays. |
| Main limitation | The study selected high-confidence cases from a larger prospective cohort, used a first-generation antibody assay rather than direct viral RNA testing for every sample, and did not itself inoculate or clone infectious virus. Anti-HCV absence in some acute resolving cases remained ambiguous. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that anti-HCV seroconversion tracked prospectively followed transfusion-associated non-A non-B hepatitis, especially chronic biopsy-supported cases, in this selected NIH cohort.
- It can answer that anti-HCV often appeared after a delay and could persist for years, especially in chronic cases.
- It can answer that anti-HCV-positive donor samples were found in most fully evaluable linked donor-recipient transmissions.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot prove infectivity of a cloned HCV genome.
- It cannot prove ordinary non-transfusion transmission routes.
- It cannot show that every non-A non-B hepatitis case is HCV.
- It cannot replace direct viral RNA, culture, or molecular-clone challenge evidence.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The paper strongly supports a prospective serology and donor-linkage rung for transfusion-associated non-A non-B hepatitis. The leap begins if antibody seroconversion is treated as equivalent to direct infectious virus recovery, cloned-genome infectivity, or proof of all hepatitis C clinical outcomes.

## What this wiki currently concludes
This source supports a narrow claim: Alter et al. 1989 found anti-HCV seroconversion in all selected chronic transfusion-associated non-A non-B hepatitis cases and linked anti-HCV-positive donors to most fully evaluable cases, while preserving assay-window, selection, and anti-HCV-negative-case limitations.
