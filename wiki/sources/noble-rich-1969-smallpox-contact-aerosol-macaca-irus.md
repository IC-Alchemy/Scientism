# Transmission of Smallpox by Contact and by Aerosol Routes in Macaca irus

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary variola contact/aerosol transmission experiment in monkeys
- Journal/year: *Bulletin of the World Health Organization*, 1969
- Authors: J. Noble, Jr. and J. A. Rich
- Source basis in hand: full-text PMC PDF

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Experimental animal inoculation, serial contact exposure, aerosol exposure, virus isolation, and serology |
| Subject / sample | *Macaca irus philippinensis* monkeys from Cebu; Harvey variola major strain propagated in chicken embryos |
| Exposure or intervention | Intranasal inoculation of index monkeys; paired contact exposure to healthy monkeys; serial contact passage; chamber aerosol exposure with upwind infected and downwind control monkeys separated from direct contact |
| Comparator / control | Healthy cage-mates or downwind exposed monkeys; separated aerosol controls with independent food, water, and equipment |
| Endpoint | Fever, exanthem, lesion count and distribution, crust virus isolation in embryonated eggs, and hemagglutination-inhibition seroconversion |
| Result | Intranasally inoculated monkeys developed a mild exanthematous variola illness. Contact animals became infected in the first study, and serial contact passage continued through several monkey passages before dying out. In two aerosol studies, downwind control monkeys developed fever and extensive rash after exposure to inoculated animals, and variola was isolated from both index and exposed monkeys. |
| Main limitation | The host model was not human smallpox: illness was milder, distribution differed from human smallpox, and serial passage died out rather than increasing virulence. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that experimentally infected *Macaca irus* could transmit variola to other monkeys by contact under the reported conditions.
- It can answer that variola could transmit in a chamber arrangement designed to separate aerosol exposure from direct contact.
- It can answer that the monkey syndrome was clinically and epidemiologically model-bound.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot prove ordinary human smallpox transmission by itself.
- It cannot establish a wild monkey reservoir.
- It cannot show that monkey smallpox was identical in severity, lesion distribution, or natural history to human smallpox.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The risk is treating monkey contact/aerosol transmission as if it fully reconstructs human smallpox epidemiology. The paper is stronger than simple inoculation because it includes contact and aerosol exposure, but it remains an experimental animal model.

## Vault conclusion
Noble and Rich 1969 supports a significant nonhuman-primate contact/aerosol transmission rung for variola, while preserving host-model limits and the absence of evidence for a naturally maintained monkey smallpox reservoir.
