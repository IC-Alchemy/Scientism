# An Assay for Circulating Antibodies to a Major Etiologic Virus of Human Non-A Non-B Hepatitis

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary recombinant-antigen antibody-assay paper for HCV and non-A non-B hepatitis
- Journal/year: *Science*, 1989
- Authors: G. Kuo, Q.-L. Choo, H. J. Alter, G. L. Gitnick, A. G. Redeker, R. H. Purcell, T. Miyamura, J. L. Dienstag, M. J. Alter, C. E. Stevens, G. E. Tegtmeier, F. Bonino, M. Colombo, W.-S. Lee, C. Kuo, K. Berger, J. R. Shuster, L. R. Overby, D. W. Bradley, and M. Houghton
- Source basis in hand: full-text *Science* article scan in the same acquired PDF packet used for the 1989 HCV clone paper, checked against DOI and PubMed metadata

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Recombinant-antigen antibody-assay development and validation using chimpanzee-proven infectious sera, matched donor-recipient transfusion samples, and clinical NANBH cohorts |
| Subject / sample | C100-3 recombinant HCV antigen expressed in yeast; sera known to transmit NANBH to chimpanzees; normal donor and non-NANBH liver-disease controls; serial sera from ten chronic post-transfusion NANBH recipients; stored matched donor sera; additional NANBH cohorts from the United States, Italy, and Japan |
| Exposure or intervention | Serum testing with a C100-3-coated microtiter assay for circulating antibodies to the HCV recombinant antigen |
| Comparator / control | SOD-related assay controls, sera from normal donors, alcoholic hepatitis, primary biliary cirrhosis, noninfectious donor samples, and prospectively studied non-HCV viral hepatitis cases |
| Endpoint | Anti-HCV antibody signal, recipient seroconversion, donor anti-HCV positivity, and association with chimpanzee-proven infectious NANBH or chronic post-transfusion NANBH |
| Result | Anti-HCV was detected in six of seven chimpanzee-proven infectious NANBH sera. In ten chronic post-transfusion NANBH cases, all recipients seroconverted and nine of ten had at least one anti-HCV-positive implicated donor. Additional reported positivity included high proportions of chronic post-transfusion NANBH cohorts from Italy and Japan, lower positivity in acute resolving cases, and substantial positivity in U.S. NANBH cases without an identifiable parenteral source. |
| Main limitation | Antibody detection is indirect, may appear after infection rather than at onset, was weaker in acute resolving disease, and cannot by itself distinguish active infectious virus from prior exposure or prove that every NANBH case was HCV. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that antibodies to recombinant C100-3 HCV antigen were strongly associated with chimpanzee-proven infectious NANBH sera.
- It can answer that anti-HCV seroconversion occurred in the ten chronic post-transfusion NANBH recipients tested in this assay-development paper.
- It can answer that anti-HCV-positive donor samples were found in most of the matched transfusion cases in the paper's selected set.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot directly detect infectious virus or prove current viremia in a sample.
- It cannot by itself prove that HCV caused every non-A non-B hepatitis case.
- It cannot replace the molecular-identification, transmission/passaging, prospective cohort, or infectious molecular-clone rungs of the HCV ladder.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The paper supports a strong antibody-association and assay-validation rung for blood-borne NANBH. The leap begins if anti-HCV antibody detection is treated as identical to infectious virus isolation, purified-clone causality, ordinary transmission, or universal attribution of all NANBH to HCV.

## Vault conclusion
This source supports a narrow claim: Kuo et al. 1989 developed a recombinant C100-3 anti-HCV assay and found anti-HCV strongly associated with chimpanzee-proven infectious NANBH and chronic post-transfusion NANBH, while preserving the limits of antibody timing, acute resolving disease, and indirect serologic inference.
