# Scobey - Poisoning as the Cause of Poliomyelitis

## Source type
Historical medical journal article, currently ingested from an OCR-style markdown rendering of a PDF excerpt.

> Warning: This page is for research analysis only. It is not medical advice.

> Note: `source_tier: tier-1` reflects historical source value as a 1950 medical article. It is not Tier 1 experimental proof that poisoning caused poliomyelitis generally.

## What the source claims
- Scobey argues that poliomyelitis research became too narrowly committed to a virus framework while neglecting poisoning and intoxication hypotheses.
- The article assembles older case reports, toxicology examples, food-poisoning episodes, and nutritional arguments to claim that many paralytic syndromes resembling poliomyelitis may be toxic in origin.
- It further argues that seasonal epidemics and gastrointestinal prodromes are at least compatible with foodborne, chemical, or environmental poisoning explanations.

## What question the source is capable of answering
This source can answer what one mid-20th-century physician argued against the dominant poliomyelitis-causation framework, and what kinds of evidence he considered relevant.

It can also document that poisoning-based interpretations were part of the historical debate.

It cannot, by itself, establish that poisoning rather than infection caused poliomyelitis generally, because most of its evidence is analogical, selective, or based on clinical resemblance rather than a decisive comparative test.

## What this wiki currently concludes
- This is an important historical dissent source for the polio branch of the vault.
- Its strongest value is methodological and historiographic: it shows how critics tried to separate observed paralysis syndromes from a single favored causal narrative.
- The article does not yet justify a wiki conclusion that poisoning was the true general cause of poliomyelitis.

## Methodology and evidence notes
- Many examples concern toxins that produced paralysis or anterior-horn lesions resembling poliomyelitis.
- That kind of resemblance can raise a causal question, but it does not by itself identify which cause operated in a given epidemic.
- Several cited episodes predate later diagnostic conventions, so disease labeling and endpoint definition remain important uncertainties.
- The current ingest is based on markdown extracted from a PDF and should be checked against a stable facsimile before relying on exact quotations.

## Open questions
