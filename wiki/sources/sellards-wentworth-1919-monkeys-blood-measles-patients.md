# Insusceptibility of Monkeys to Inoculation with Blood from Measles Patients

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary negative-result paper for measles blood-inoculation claims in monkeys
- Journal/year: *Bulletin of the Johns Hopkins Hospital*, 1919
- Authors: Andrew Watson Sellards and John A. Wentworth
- Source basis in hand: full text from Internet Archive volume OCR and PDF

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Animal inoculation and clinical-observation study |
| Subject / sample | Three adult rhesus monkeys; blood from adult measles patients aged roughly 20 to 30 years |
| Exposure or intervention | Intraperitoneal inoculation with defibrinated or citrated blood taken within the first 24 hours after rash appearance; two monkeys were reinjected, and one normal monkey received repeated blood inoculations over three days from fresh cases |
| Comparator / control | No sham-inoculated monkey control group; one inoculated monkey's blood was later injected into a human volunteer as a downstream infectivity check |
| Endpoint | Measles-like clinical disease in monkeys, temperature, leukocyte count, rash, Koplik spots, conjunctivitis, rhinitis, coryza, malaise, appetite, and secondary human-volunteer symptoms after monkey blood transfer |
| Result | The authors reported that the three monkeys did not develop symptoms diagnostic or suggestive of measles. Two reinjected animals also failed to develop symptoms. Blood taken from one monkey 11 days after inoculation and injected into a human volunteer produced no symptoms. |
| Main limitation | The study tested blood inoculation, not respiratory secretions or ordinary respiratory transmission. Its negative result is route- and material-specific and does not by itself refute measles transmission by nasopharyngeal secretions or later tissue-culture measles-virus evidence. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that, in this small series, blood from early measles cases did not produce recognizable measles-like illness in rhesus monkeys under the tested inoculation conditions.
- It can answer that the authors regarded fever without leukopenia, rash without Koplik spots, and absence of respiratory/mucosal signs as insufficient for a positive measles diagnosis in monkeys.
- It can answer that a downstream human-volunteer injection with blood from one inoculated monkey did not produce symptoms.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot answer whether nasopharyngeal secretions transmit measles to monkeys.
- It cannot answer whether measles virus can be propagated in tissue culture.
- It cannot answer ordinary human respiratory transmission.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The leap runs in two directions. Later positive monkey or culture work can erase this blood-route failure; counter-narratives can overread it as a general failure of measles causation. The narrow finding is a failed blood-inoculation endpoint.

## Vault conclusion
Sellards and Wentworth 1919 is a primary negative-result boundary paper. It supports a narrow claim about failed measles blood inoculation in rhesus monkeys, not a broad conclusion about all measles-virus evidence.
