# The Physical State of Human Papillomavirus Type 16 DNA in Benign and Malignant Genital Tumours

## Source type
- Role in this vault: primary HPV16 restriction-map and physical-state paper comparing benign and malignant genital tumor material
- Journal/year: *Journal of General Virology*, 1985
- Authors: Matthias Durst, Andreas Kleinheinz, Marlies Hotz, and Lutz Gissmann
- Source basis in hand: full-text archived PDF from Microbiology Society/CiteseerX via the Internet Archive

## Evidence fields
| Field | Extraction |
|---|---|
| Study type | Molecular restriction mapping and physical-state analysis of HPV16 DNA in benign and malignant genital tumors |
| Subject / sample | Cloned HPV16 DNA; six benign HPV16-positive genital tumors; four malignant tumors including cervical carcinomas and Bowen's disease; detailed analysis of cervical carcinoma WV2965 and matched lymphocyte DNA |
| Exposure or intervention | Restriction enzyme mapping, Southern blot hybridization, CsCl/ethidium bromide gradient centrifugation, two-dimensional gel electrophoresis, and cloning of virus/cell junction fragments |
| Comparator / control | HPV6b DNA for colinearity; benign versus malignant tumors; episomal versus linear/high-molecular-weight DNA behavior; matched patient lymphocyte DNA for cellular-junction comparison |
| Endpoint | HPV16 genome size/map, episomal versus integrated physical state, and cloned virus/cell junction fragments |
| Result | HPV16 genome size was estimated at 7.8 +/- 0.1 kb and colinear with HPV6b. Six benign tumors contained HPV16 exclusively as 8 kb circles. Four malignant tumors showed evidence of integration; one cervical carcinoma and one Bowen's disease case also contained oligomeric episomal HPV DNA. WV2965 had HPV16 integrated as head-to-tail tandem repeats at more than one site, with three cloned virus/cell junction fragments. |
| Main limitation | The sample set was small and selected. Physical-state evidence does not establish whether integration caused malignant transformation or occurred after tumor development. |

## What the experiment can answer
- It can answer that HPV16 DNA was episomal in the six tested benign lesions.
- It can answer that HPV16 DNA in four tested malignant lesions showed integration-compatible patterns, including cloned virus/cell junctions in one cervical carcinoma.
- It can answer that HPV16 integration was not the only physical state observed in malignant material, because some malignant samples also contained episomal molecules.

## What it cannot answer
- It cannot determine whether HPV16 integration is a cause or consequence of malignant transformation.
- It cannot quantify general cervical-cancer risk.
- It cannot prove that HPV16 infection is necessary or sufficient for cervical cancer.

## Later inferential-leap risk
The source itself states that whether HPV16/HPV18 integration is a causative event or a consequence of tumor-cell DNA rearrangement is an open question. The leap begins if integration evidence is retold as proof of oncogenic causality.

## Vault conclusion
This source supports a narrow claim: Durst et al. 1985 showed episomal HPV16 DNA in selected benign lesions and integration-compatible HPV16 DNA in selected malignant lesions, while explicitly leaving causal direction unresolved.
