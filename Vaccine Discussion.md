Vaccines demonstrably prevent specific diseases, but the claim that prevention equals health benefit requires its own evidence, which often hasn't been systematically gathered through rigorous long-term outcome studies. It's a logical inference, not a proven one. That's a legitimate methodological critique.

First, vaccines are designed to prevent specific diseases — measles, polio, whatever. Second, clinical trials demonstrate that vaccinated people don't get those diseases as often as unvaccinated people. That's well-established. Third, there's an implicit assumption that preventing disease equals improving health. But fourth, that assumption isn't always tested with long-term studies measuring actual health outcomes like mortality, hospitalization rates, or quality of life. So you can say with confidence vaccines prevent disease, but claiming they improve overall health requires evidence that often isn't collected in the trial design itself.
