Introduction. Up to now, hygiene has been able to draw relatively little support from the progress made in the knowledge of pathogenic organisms. The reason for this is that the great majority of questions concerning pathogenic microorganisms that are relevant to hygiene can only be answered using reliable methods for separating the various types of these organisms. For example, hygiene is not concerned with finding out whether fungi, bacteria and other lower organisms are present in this or that soil or drinking water, but rather whether there are any that can cause disease. And if it has been possible to prove the presence of a notoriously harmful or merely suspect organism, then it is a matter of studying it in all its circumstances, separated from others that must have a disturbing and confusing effect on observation, of investigating its living conditions, its history of development, and everything that is beneficial or detrimental to it. This knowledge can only be gained with the help of continuous cultures of the individual species, so-called pure cultures, for which there are as yet no reliable methods that are applicable in all directions. I have made many efforts to fill this gap and have finally arrived at results that are certainly capable of and in need of much improvement, but which have already proven themselves in their current form in the investigations into infectious diseases and disinfection carried out by the Health Office. Partly in order to make these methods, which are capable of very versatile use and exploitation, accessible to a wider circle of people, and partly to facilitate the understanding of the work carried out using these methods and which will be published in these journals, they will be described below.

Description of the task. In general, the study of lower organisms for the purposes of health care will have to take the following points into account. First of all, it must be determined whether the organisms in question are pathogenic at all, ie are capable of causing disease. This is followed by proof of their infectiousness, ie transferability to other, previously healthy individuals, either those belonging to the same species as the individual who was first spontaneously affected by the disease or artificially infected, or to those who are

Furthermore, the manner in which pathogenic organisms enter the animal body, their behaviour outside the body in the air, in the water and in the soil must be followed and finally the influence exerted on them by development-inhibiting and destructive substances must be determined. Their stay in the body is of interest to hygiene only insofar as it can provide information about the type of infection, e.g. localisation of pathogenic organisms in the intestine, transfer to the blood, formation of permanent conditions within the body. Determination of pathogenic properties. Let us now turn to each of these tasks, first of all to determining the pathogenic properties and the infectivity of the microorganisms.

Some researchers still claim that bacteria are present in the blood and tissues of the healthy body; however, this claim is not based on direct evidence using a microscope, but partly on theoretical assumptions and partly on experiments on the putrefaction capacity of healthy organ parts isolated under antiseptic conditions. However, there are very serious objections to these experiments, and a discussion of these would take us too far afield here. One thing is certain: using a microscope and with the aid of examination methods that can still identify isolated bacteria with certainty in animal organs, it has not yet been possible to detect them in the blood and tissues of healthy individuals in such a way that there can be no doubt about their presence there, even during life.[[]]

As soon as bacteria, and the same applies to other microorganisms, are found inside the organs, be it in the blood or lymph vessels or in the tissue itself, in conditions that can only occur in the living body, or if the unmistakable influence of the microorganisms on the tissue affected by their invasion can be observed, e.g. necrosis of the cells located in a certain area, accumulation of round cells in the vicinity, penetration of foreign organisms into the cells, etc., then such microorganisms must be considered pathogenic, at least they must appear suspicious and call for further investigation and clarification of the findings.

It is more difficult to decide whether microorganisms found on the surface of the body and on its mucous membranes are pathogenic. Here, only the mass occurrence and the differences in shape between the presumably pathogenic and the organisms known to be harmle[[]]ss, which usually live in or on the body, can be decisive. Unfortunately, too little attention has been paid to these harmless parasites up to now; a deficiency that is particularly noticeable in relation to intestinal disorders and requires a certain degree of caution with regard to all information about pathogenic bacteria in the intestine until all doubt has been excluded that confusion with habitual inhabitants of the intestine, which

Detection of pathogenic microorganisms. When it comes to finding the pathogenic organisms suspected in the diseased body, first of all bacteria, one encounters the most significant, and in some cases insurmountable, obstacles in the usual microscopic examination, which is carried out without special preparations or tricks. For although some pathogenic bacteria are so distinguished by their size, characteristic shape and mobility that they are not easy to overlook, others have such a simple shape and are so small that, when mixed with the similarly shaped decay products of tissue cells, it is impossible to distinguish them from them. Fortunately, however, bacteria have a property that makes it possible to overcome all these difficulties. It is their high ability to absorb certain dyes, especially aniline dyes. However, since the fluids in which the bacteria are found, such as blood, mucus, tissue fluids, etc., when immediately mixed with Amlin dyes, give rise to precipitates which are also colored and which can either simulate bacteria by their granular or thread-like appearance or conceal the existing bacteria by their voluminous masses, further preparations are required in order to make the bacteria clearly visible using the dye solutions. When examining the colored objects with the microscope, a very special device for illuminating the preparation is also required if the advantages of the staining method are to be fully realized.

Some years ago I described in detail the most suitable methods for detecting bacteria when they are found in liquids or in animal tissues, and published some of them in a paper published in F. Colin's Contributions to Plant Biology, Volume 2, Issue 51), and some in a paper on wound infections.2) Regarding the details of these methods, I must refer to the papers mentioned in order not to go into too much detail; here I will only discuss those points which have been improved since then or which have been subject to misunderstanding and therefore require correction.

Microorganisms in liquids. The method of identifying bacteria in liquids, e.g. in blood, biter, tissue juice, using dyes, consists in spreading the liquid in the thinnest possible layer on the cover glass, drying it and then exposing it to the effect of the dye solutions. If the liquids containing bacteria do not contain any protein or only a very small amount, then the staining is almost always excellent. However, if they contain more or less protein, then for a long time after drying, the layer does not adhere so firmly that it is not largely softened by the dye solution, torn up and even partially washed off the cover glass. The protein has not become insoluble through drying either; most of it passes into the dye solution and forms precipitates with the dye that cling firmly to the cover glass, covering everything and making it unrecognizable. When Ehrlich's investigations 1 ) became known, the excellent results he had obtained on heated blood preparations for distinguishing between the various granulated blood cells prompted the study of the effect of heat on bacterial preparations. Ehrlich exposed the cover glasses with the dried blood layer to high temperatures (120°—130°) for one to several hours. Such intensive exposure to heat makes the blood layer completely solid and insoluble, but the bacteria, as the experiments carried out have shown, lose their ability to absorb dye. For our purposes, however, it was sufficient to allow the heat to act only long enough for the protein bodies to become insoluble, and this can be achieved in a much shorter time. If the cover glasses are exposed to a temperature of 120°—130° for just a few minutes, the layer is already so solid that it no longer forms precipitates with the dye solutions and can be dyed very well. The length of time the heat is needed cannot be specified with any precision. Sometimes the preparation is heated sufficiently after just 2 minutes, sometimes only after 5-10 minutes. It should also be noted that some bacteria, such as anthrax bacilli, appear somewhat different when they are first heated and then stained; they look thinner and more delicate than when they are stained with glycerine brown, and they do not show the structure that is so characteristic of the anthrax bacilli as clearly. I would like to take this opportunity to point out that slight differences, similar to those just discussed, mainly in the width of the bacteria, can be seen when the preparations are prepared in different ways or stained with different dyes. For this reason, only preparations prepared using a completely identical procedure can be used for comparison. If, for example, one wants to make the characteristic structure of the anthrax bacilli, which allows an unmistakable diagnosis of the same, most clearly visible, then, as already mentioned, it is most appropriate to stain with glycerine brown. It is used continuously in the work on infectious diseases in the health department and has become indispensable. There is another reason why I would consider Ehrlich's method of investigation to be important, which relates to infectious diseases. Ehrlich has provided evidence that, among the cellular elements of the blood, which were generally considered to be of equal value, differences can be detected with the aid of dyes, which is as much as with the aid of chemical reactions, which must lead to the assumption that these differences are related to the origin and physiological significance of the cells. But what does the differentiating coloring of blood cells have to do with infectious diseases? Simply that in one or more groups of infectious diseases the pathogens could possibly occur in a form similar to the white blood cells, for example in an amoeba-like form, and that in this case it would be of great value to have reliable distinguishing features, such as those undoubtedly provided by Ehrlich's method of staining. It is certainly a one-sided, although currently generally accepted, opinion that all as yet unknown infectious agents must be bacteria. Why should other microorganisms not be just as capable of leading a parasitic life in the animal body? I do not want to claim that these are only amoeba-like creatures. Other members of the kingdom of protists are also suspect.

Photographic images of microorganisms. Photographic images of microorganisms are of the greatest importance for the study of microorganisms. If a purely objective, bias-free approach is necessary anywhere, it is in this area. But the exact opposite has taken place so far, and nowhere are there more subjectively colored views and, as a result, more differences of opinion than in the study of pathogenic microorganisms. No one will deny that the difference in the understanding of the relationships of one and the same object is almost always due to the fact that this object appeared to the first researcher under a different image than the second. Just remember that we are only dealing with microscopic objects and that when using a microscope two observers cannot look at the same object at the same time and come to an agreement about it, but that one after the other sees the object in question and, as every microscopist knows, the slightest displacement of the micrometer screw results in objects such as bacteria either disappearing completely from the field of vision or appearing with completely different outlines and shadows. Nevertheless, communication about the object seen is more easily possible when it is observed with one and the same instrument, that is, with the same lighting, the same lens system and at the same magnification. However, if the many conditions under which the microscopic image is created are different, for example if the microscope is in the same position, the microscope may not be the same. For example, if one observer carries out his examination with a narrow illuminating aperture, another with a wide one, one with a weak eyepiece, another with a strong one, etc., or if the preparation and coloring of the object are unequal, if it is further immersed in liquids with different refractive powers, how can it be surprising if one microscopist claims to have seen an object quite differently, perhaps thicker or thinner, more or less shiny, or if he possibly does not find it at all and therefore denies its existence? And how can the observation error, whether it lies on one side or the other, be proven in such cases among the many possibilities mentioned above? Was it due to the preparation or the handling of the microscope that the observers came to different conclusions about the same object? It will almost never be possible to decide this without other means; each of the disputants naturally sticks to his own opinion, and medical science does not know whom to believe. Transmissibility of pathogenic microorganisms. The methods discussed so far prove the presence of microorganisms in the animal body, and if the examination shows that the parasites are present in large numbers or that they have caused irritation, necrosis, etc. of the affected tissue, then their pathogenic properties are established. Secondly, we are now interested in the question of whether the microorganisms identified as pathogenic are also infectious, and can be transmitted from one body to another. The two terms pathogenic and infectious must not be confused with one another. It is easy to imagine organisms that are able to migrate into the animal body and make it ill, i.e. are pathogenic, but do not have the ability to pass directly from one body to another and make it ill, or to infect it. Assuming that intermittent disease is a bacterial disease, which requires further proof, it would be an excellent example of the existence of a pathogenic but not infectious microorganism. The properties of pathogenicity and infection are therefore not identical, and if a parasite is recognized as pathogenic, it must also be determined experimentally whether it is also transmissible or not. If the procedure used for this is to be successful, it will have to be as close as possible to the conditions occurring in nature, a rule that was ignored in the early days of experimental treatment of infectious diseases and is often still ignored today. In the most primitive way, attempts have been made to transmit diseases that have previously only been observed in humans to dogs, cats, rabbits, guinea pigs, and the like. Gradually, however, experience has shown that it is not at all unimportant which animal species is chosen for the infection experiments and that, moreover, the method of transmission has the greatest influence on the success of the experiment. To discuss all these factors in detail would take us too far afield here, and only the most important aspects can be briefly highlighted in order to characterize the principles on which we proceeded in our work.
As far as the choice of experimental animals is concerned, it is advisable to initially use animals of the same species as those from which the infectious material originates. Only if this is not possible should related species be used. If human infectious diseases are involved, then the animals closest to humans, monkeys, should also be used, as the striking example of recurrens shows, which has so far not been able to be transferred to any other animal species than monkeys, but to them it can be transferred easily and safely. However, the experiment must not stop at the transfer of the infectious agent to individuals of the same or related species: the reaction of as many different animal species as possible to the infectious agent must then be tested. Pure culture. After the presence of pathogenic microorganisms in the animal body, after their ability to reproduce in the body and their transmissibility to other individuals have been established, the most important task, and the one of most interest to hygiene, remains to investigate their living conditions. As already emphasized at the beginning of this work, this task can only be solved with the help of pure culture, and therefore it is not an exaggeration to say that the focus of all research into infectious diseases lies in pure culture.
Because the importance of pure culture has long been understood, all those who research in the field of infectious diseases have made every effort to perfect the methods of pure culture. The results of the more recent and recent work, however, prove most evidently that we have not progressed very far beyond the first feeble attempts. At most we have learned to discard the most serious errors, and even these are not always the case.
The essentials of pure culture as it is currently practiced can be summarised roughly as follows.
A sterilized, suitable nutrient fluid is placed in a disinfected vessel, which is sealed with disinfected cotton wool to make it "fungus-tight," and this is "inoculated" with the substance containing the microorganisms to be cultivated. Once they have multiplied, the inoculation can be carried out from the first vessel using disinfected instruments into a second, similarly prepared vessel, and so on. In short, it is almost the same process as when an infectious disease is transmitted from one animal to another.
Of course, some assumptions are made, namely, that the culture vessel is really disinfected. But how harmless this disinfection was sometimes imagined to be is demonstrated by the dispute between Pasteur and Bastian about spontaneous generation and the well-known argument of the former against the latter: "Flambez-vous vos vases avant de vous en servir,"1 which Bastian had to deny.
Secondly, that the disinfected cotton wool does indeed seal tightly, which according to the investigations of N aegeli 2 ) cannot be assumed to be valid for all cases.

Here we will assume that a suitable nutrient solution has been found and that it only needs to be sterilized. Anyone who has had the opportunity to work with hay infusion, meat extract or malt extract solutions as nutrient fluids knows what difficulties and dangers this work entails for the success of the experiment. In botany and zoology it is a generally followed principle to describe all living things that were previously unknown, to name them and to register them provisionally as independent species. It has, however, sometimes happened that individual species that were considered independent have later turned out to be forms that belonged to the evolutionary circle of an already known species. But far more often it has been necessary, with more precise investigation and the application of more refined methods and better instruments, to divide a species that was previously considered to be a single one into several. Strangely enough, this tried and tested and generally valid principle of keeping all new forms that differ significantly from one another in their properties apart until their belonging together has been irrefutably proven has been frequently deviated from in the field of microorganisms, particularly in the field of bacteria. From the beginning of bacterial research up to the most recent times, from Häller to Naege 1 and Buchner, we have encountered attempts to throw together bacteria, which are undeniably very different in their properties, without looking at them, and to make a single or at most a few species out of them. If it should really be possible one day to transform bacterial species by transferring or breeding from one known form into another, then it is surely still time to combine these forms, which have been shown to belong together, into one species. This proof has not yet been provided, and there is not the slightest reason to deviate from the maxims of general natural research in the theory of bacteria. Even if a few too many species were initially assumed, this would not be detrimental to science, but if the usefulness and necessity of researching the various forms of bacteria and making them accessible to science is dismissed from the outset, this would put a stop to all further research and progress in this field, and this is certainly to the greatest detriment of the development of this young and promising science. Truth and knowledge will undoubtedly finally break through here, just as in other areas of knowledge, and throw all untenable hypotheses into disarray. But as so often happens, here too, true progress, which only moves along the path of laborious and slow research, can be pushed into the background for a while by promising theories that seem to solve even the most difficult problems with ease, and even if this does not cause any lasting detriment to science, the wrong direction can still cause great harm.that for a time it will gain influence in some of the most important areas of health care and that its teachings will be translated into practice.

Before I close the chapter on the no-culture, I would like to defend myself against an objection that I will certainly not be spared. It will be objected that my pure culture method is actually nothing new and that it is already well known to cultivate bacteria on potatoes and in gelatine. That is true. The sample is spread line by line on slides coated with nutrient gelatine, so that the individual particle corners are not too crowded together. I have to point out the points to be observed beforehand. The microscopist, while observing an object, almost continuously sets the
Micrometer screw in motion, sometimes he moves the tube closer, sometimes he moves it away from the point in view and in this way not only receives a visual impression of the object in question and everything that lies in the same plane as it, but he also immediately orientates himself about what lies immediately above and below it. Photography has to forego this advantage, because the photographic image can only ever reproduce a single, very thin layer of the preparation. What lies exactly in this plane appears with sharp outlines, everything else, depending on how far or far away it is from the focused plane, appears unclear, even further it looks blurred and the objects furthest from the fixed point in the direction of the microscope axis only create a shadow in the image. At first glance, the photographic image of preparations that have a certain thickness, i.e. of all tissue sections, therefore appears somewhat strange. Plate XI, No. 32 and the other photograms in this plate, as well as the first three in the next plate, owe their origin to a case of anthrax in humans, which arouses interest in many respects. In my previous sphere of activity I had the opportunity not infrequently to observe anthrax infection in humans. The form in which the disease appeared was almost always a considerable swelling and bruising, which spread more or less from the site of infection on the face, neck, forearm or hand. When the patient sought medical attention, the skin at the site of infection itself was usually already extensively gangrenous, sometimes surrounded by blue-red or blackish blisters. The diagnosis could only be made with certainty by demonstrating the anthrax bacilli at the site of infection and by successfully infecting test animals. The case we are dealing with here is quite different from these forms of anthrax. A strong cattle maid from a place where anthrax caused havoc among sheep and not infrequently among cattle every year had a strange tumor develop in the upper sternal region over the course of eight days from a small scratch wound. The shape of this tumor can most easily be compared to that of a smallpox, which has assumed quite unusual dimensions. In the middle there is a deep depression of a blackish color, which is surrounded by a broad, yellowish-white bulge. The latter has a fairly firm consistency and is radially furrowed; on the outer edge the tumor is still covered with epidermis; towards the inside the epidermis has detached. The tumor mass thus exposed secretes an almost water-clear fluid in such quantities that it drips down drop by drop. The size of the tumor corresponds approximately to that of a small potato cut in half. This disease, which was quite peculiar in its appearance, did not even remotely resemble the known symptoms of anthrax disease, especially since the tumor was clearly set off from the surrounding area, which was not reddened or otherwise changed. However, when some of the substance was scraped off the surface of the nodule and examined under a microscope, it revealed numerous other bacteria, namely micrococci,

The patient appeared very weak, complained of severe chest pains and had a body temperature of 40.9 °C. I can be brief about the further course of events. The lump was immediately excised, the surgical wound was treated with a 1% carbolic acid solution and the surrounding area was also injected with 2% carbolic acid using a Pravaz syringe. The disease then healed quickly. After the excision, the tumor was immediately placed in alcohol and further microscopic examination completely confirmed the preliminary diagnosis of anthrax. The vaccinated animals also succumbed to actual anthrax, the mice the next day and the rabbit the day after that; they all had greatly enlarged spleens and countless anthrax bacilli in the spleen, lungs and heart blood. Further vaccinations were then carried out on these animals, which caused typical anthrax in the usual way. The form of anthrax described here seems to occur only very rarely in humans. The authors do, however, list a so-called smallpox form of anthrax, which behaves in a fundamentally different way; it is said to be a pea- or bean-sized blister of cellular structure, with a depression in the middle at the level of an erysipelatous tumor. In our case, the smallpox, if I may call it that, was considerably larger and the surroundings completely unchanged, so it cannot be subsumed under the smallpox form of anthrax as it is usually called.
